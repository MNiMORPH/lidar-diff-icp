# Low herbaceous vegetation and airborne lidar ground surfaces: a literature review

Prepared 2026-10-19 for the Elba floodplain two-epoch (Nov 2008 / May 2021) differencing problem. Every number below is pasted from a retrieved source; numbers taken from an abstract or landing page rather than full text are marked "(abstract)". Sources I could not open are flagged in the table and are characterised only by title or by what a retrieved paper says about them.

## Summary

Airborne lidar reads the ground too high under dense herbaceous cover, by an amount that scales with canopy height. The best-measured systems are salt marshes: Rogers et al. (2016) found a bias-to-height ratio of 34% across four Cape Cod marshes, with 0.23 m under Spartina alterniflora and 0.41 m under its tall form; Ewald (2013) found 37–49 cm under freshwater Carex sedges and 13–20 cm under wet pasture grasses, all from winter flights. A senescent-season flight cut the Cape Cod bias from 0.14 to 0.04 m, but flattened dead stems still bias returns, and dormant-season California marshes retained 0.23–0.28 m bias. The mechanism is the discrete-return dead zone (1.5–3 m on 2008-era sensors), which forces a single return per pulse whose timing sits roughly one third of the way up the canopy. Fixed low percentiles and minimum-bin gridding reduce but do not remove the bias; RTK-calibrated class or NDVI corrections reach 7–10 cm RMSE. No study treats reed canary grass, hay-field phenology, or thatch.

## 1. Magnitude and sign of the ground-elevation bias

**Sign.** With one exception, every study retrieved finds lidar ground above the true surface under herbaceous cover. The exception is Su & Bork (2006, PE&RS), whose Alberta parkland DEM "over-estimated [elevations] in aspen forest (0.20 m) while those in lowland meadows were under-estimated (0.22 m)"; they attribute part of the meadow sign to cattle-hoof micro-topography and note that "none of the open grasslands had an ideal signed error of 0 m, potentially indicative of a bias contributed by the GPS control during lidar sampling". Their survey was flown "03 October 2000 during leaf-on conditions" at 0.28–1.35 points/m² with overall signed error +0.02 m and RMSE 0.59 m. I treat their negative meadow sign as unresolved rather than as evidence that grass can read low.

**Salt-marsh grasses and rushes (the best-constrained numbers).** Hladik & Alber (2012, RSE) found that "DEM mean vertical errors for different cover classes ranged from 0.03 to 0.25 m in comparison to the RTK ground truth data, with the larger offsets for taller vegetation", overall 0.10 ± 0.12 m (SD), RMSE 0.16 m, on an Optech Gemini at 125 kHz (abstract; the per-class table was not retrievable). Rogers, Parrish, Ward & Burdick (2016, J. Coastal Res. SI 76) report, for a 20 July 2010 flight over four Cape Cod marshes with 2,648 RTK points and IDW gridding: all vegetation 0.14 m (σ 0.17 m, RMS 0.22 m); Spartina alterniflora 0.23 m (σ 0.20 m); S. patens 0.05 m; Distichlis spicata 0.06 m; Salicornia spp. 0.05 m. By S. alterniflora growth form the bias was 0.12 m (short form, <0.5 m), 0.23 m (medium, 0.5–1 m) and 0.41 m (tall, >1 m). Mean vegetation height was 0.46 m, and "The overall bias to height ratio was 34% for all vegetation species at the four field sites", 35% for S. alterniflora, S. patens and Distichlis and 25% for Salicornia. Buffington, Dugger, Thorne & Takekawa (2016, RSE), across 17 Pacific-coast marshes, measured uncorrected mean error 0.208 m (SD 0.109) and RMSE 0.231 m (SD 0.010) overall; per site the mean error ran from 0.016 m (Bandon) to 0.419 m (Grays Harbor, dominated by Carex lyngbyei), with San Francisco Bay Salicornia/Spartina foliosa sites at 0.228–0.282 m and southern California short-form sites at 0.082–0.154 m. Medeiros et al. (2015, Remote Sensing; abstract) report raw mean error 0.61 ± 0.24 m and RMSE 0.65 m in the Juncus-dominated lower Apalachicola marsh. Fernandez-Nunez et al. (2017, J. Coastal Conservation; abstract) state that "the true accuracy of supplied (unmodified) DTMs can be >0.5 m in saltmarshes dominated by dense vegetation such as Spartina densiflora". Montané & Torres (2006, PE&RS; abstract) found a South Carolina marsh overestimated "by an overall average of only 7 cm" with "little effect from the dominant macrophyte vegetation". Schmid et al. (2011, J. Coastal Res.; not opened) is reported by Ewald (2013) to have "found a mean bias of 29.7 cm in Juncus roemerianus environments" and by Buffington et al. (2016) as 0.15 m for South Carolina.

**Freshwater and brackish sedge, rush and pasture (closest analogue to a Minnesota sedge meadow).** Ewald (2013, MS thesis, Oregon State) compared winter-flown OLC lidar (Leica ALS50 Phase II, 7.2 returns/m², open-ground RMSE 4.5 cm) with survey-grade GPS in twelve Oregon tidal-wetland and pasture associations. Minimum-bin DEM discrepancies (mean ± SE, cm; bare-earth DEM in parentheses): Carex lyngbyei marsh 48.8 ± 4.4 (45.1); Carex obnupta wet pasture 36.6 ± 3.6 (39.4); C. obnupta with Oenanthe 19.5 ± 3.0 (21.5); Juncus balticus moist pasture 19.6 ± 3.3 (20.0); Argentina pacifica high marsh/pasture 18.6 ± 2.9 (23.2); J. balticus high marsh 18.2 ± 2.6 (21.0); tall fescue (Festuca arundinacea) pasture 16.9 ± 2.7 (20.5); creeping bentgrass (Agrostis stolonifera) wetland 15.7 ± 2.6 (19.4); mixed non-native pasture 13.3 ± 2.8 (15.9); Deschampsia cespitosa 11.8 ± 3.0 (13.5); Distichlis spicata 10.6 ± 3.4 (12.0); low-marsh Deschampsia/succulent mix 10.4 ± 2.4 (12.3). "All vegetation associations have mean LIDAR-GPS discrepancy estimates that are statistically different from zero (p-value < 0.01)". In a 1.3 m Carex obnupta stand "The lowest LIDAR return for this area was around 95 cm above the measured ground elevation". Phalaris arundinacea occurred in 20 of the quadrats but was not a dominant association, so no reed-canary-grass value is reported.

**Boreal wetland.** Hopkinson et al. (2005, ISPRS Archives XXXVI-8/W2; the Can. J. Remote Sensing version was not opened), Utikuma, Alberta, August 2002, Optech ALTM 2050, >3 points/m² (ground class 0.5–1.3/m²), Table 1 raw ground-classified errors: grass and herbs +0.02 m (SD 0.10, not significant); aquatic vegetation +0.15 m (SD 0.22); low shrubs +0.06 m (SD 0.12); highway 0.00 m (SD 0.07). The aquatic-class error is attributed to saturated ground rather than canopy, and laser penetration into the grass/herb canopy "ranged from 0.10 m (33%, P < 0.05) for the grass and herb class to 0.84 m (22%, P < 0.10) in the tall shrub class".

**Upland grass, crops, shrub-steppe.** Hodgson & Bresnahan (2004, PE&RS 70(3):331–339; abstract-level), March 2000 lidar, low grass 8 cm or less and high grass up to 90 cm: RMSE "from a low of 17 to 19 cm (pavement, low grass, and evergreen forests) to a high of 26 cm (deciduous forests)"; the high-grass value was not captured. Hodgson et al. (2005, PE&RS 71(7):817–823; abstract), leaf-off: RMSE "from 14.5 cm to 36.1 cm", largest in scrub-shrub, elevation under-predicted on average (to −23.6 cm under pine). Spaete et al. (2011, Remote Sensing Letters 2(4):317–326; abstract-level), sagebrush steppe: RMSE 0.072–0.220 m, mean error −0.154 to 0.017 m, "largest errors associated with herbaceous cover and steep slopes". Tinkham et al. (2012, Can. J. For. Res.) cite their 2011 DEM "RMSEs of 0.13 m in forb meadows to 0.30 m under coniferous forest canopies". Kucharczyk, Hugenholtz & Zou (2018, J. Unmanned Vehicle Systems; abstract): UAV-lidar VVA 0.24 m, median errors differing among 10 of 15 vegetation-type pairs, reportedly above zero for all types except short grass (0–0.3 m) and conifers. Anderson et al. (2024, Remote Sensing 16:384), discrete-return UAS lidar over Juncus roemerianus: ground RMSE 49 cm at 24–72 m AGL and 17 cm at 96–120 m AGL; "Increased erect canopy height values were related to higher LiDAR ground height estimates". Tienaho et al. (2025, Scand. J. Forest Res.; abstract): terrestrial-scan DTMs "on average, 8–13 cm lower" after boreal ground vegetation burned. No airborne study giving per-crop or stubble ground bias was found; Cobby et al. (2001, ISPRS J.; snippet only) retrieved crop heights to about 1.2 m, and the ASPRS standard places "crop land" in the vegetated accuracy class (Section 2).

## 2. Seasonality, phenology and acquisition specifications

**Peak growth versus senescence, same marshes.** Rogers et al. (2016) re-flew the Cape Cod marshes on 5 May 2011 "under senescent conditions and 9 months after the NCALM flight" (Optech Gemini, 3.93 pts/m²). "the spring dataset resulted in measurements closer to ground (bias of 0.04 m, σ = 0.06 m) because senescent vegetation was flattened or removed over the winter", against 0.14 m in July; S. alterniflora locations "exhibited the most significant increase in elevation bias of 0.18 m" between the two flights, while "very little change or slight negative change was observed for the other high marsh species". They caution that "senesced vegetation from the previous growing season also appears to impact the DRL pulse returns" and that "The weight of the snow/ice sometimes compresses or strips the vegetation (i.e., Spartina alterniflora stalks) to the ground line (Ewanchuk and Bertness, 2004)". A July-minus-May difference map correlated with the July lidar errors at r² = 0.91 and with vegetation height at r² = 0.59.

**Dormant-season flights that stayed biased.** Buffington et al. (2016) flew San Francisco Bay between February and April 2010 and still measured mean errors of 0.228–0.282 m under Salicornia pacifica (mean height 20 cm) and Spartina foliosa; they write that "Even lidar collected during periods of seasonally low biomass in tidal marshes can exhibit significant (>20 cm) vertical errors (Schmid et al., 2011)" and that "lidar was acquired in different seasons which may also explain regional differences in initial error". Ewald's (2013) Oregon data "was collected during the winter, to minimize the influence of vegetation" and still gave the 10–49 cm offsets above. Medeiros et al. (2022, IEEE JSTARS) acquired all lidar, ground truth and imagery in March to eliminate "seasonal effects associated with the growth and recession cycle of the marsh grasses" and still describe "a persistent high bias ... largely attributed to the dense grasses over the salt marsh surface". Su & Bork (2006) flew in early October when "lowland meadows typically contained abundant senescent plant residue" and shrubs "had experienced significant leaf loss, potentially increasing the likelihood of the lidar signal originating from nearer the ground surface".

**Lodging and compression.** A 2026 Applied Sciences study (16:8428) of lodged S. alterniflora found lodged stands "mainly concentrated between absolute elevations of approximately 16.0 and 17.2 m" as "a more single-layered distribution", against upright stands extending above 18 m. May et al. (2025, The Cryosphere) note that "dense ground vegetation could further introduce error into the snow-on DTM by compacting beneath the snowpack or elevating snow on top of dense vegetation". No study quantifies the thickness of a winter-flattened thatch or its lidar offset.

**Acquisition specifications.** The USGS Lidar Base Specification (2025 rev. A, Collection Requirements) states "Leaf-off vegetation conditions are preferred", leaf-on collections "shall be approved by the USGS–NGP/3DEP", "Ground conditions will be snow free", "free of extensive flooding or any other type of inundation", and "Penetration to the ground shall be adequate to produce an accurate and reliable bare-earth surface for the prescribed QL". It does not define leaf-off in terms of herbaceous cover. The NRCan Federal Airborne LiDAR Data Acquisition Guideline (v3.1, 2022, Section 6.2.1) says the same: "Leaf-off is a preferred vegetation condition, since it increases penetration to the ground". The ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards (Edition 2, 2023) compute NVA on "traditional open (bare soil, sand, rocks, and short grass) and urban ... terrain" and VVA on "all types of vegetated terrain, including tall weeds, crop land, brush, and fully forested areas"; VVA "is exempted from pass/fail testing criteria" and is reported as a 95th percentile because vegetated errors "do not necessarily follow a normal distribution". Tall grass and crops are therefore where accuracy is reported but not enforced. The 2008 Southeast Minnesota metadata (NOAA InPort 68818) gives 18–24 November 2008, Optech ALTM Gemini and Leica ALS50-2, 1.0 m nominal spacing and CVA 0.36 m at 95%, and does not address leaf-off.

## 3. Return structure in short vegetation

**Why low vegetation yields single returns.** Discrete-return receivers cannot register a second echo within a fixed interval after the first. Rogers et al. (2016): "discrete-return systems typically have a significant 'dead zone' following each detected return ... a consecutive return typically cannot be detected for a surface 1.2–3.0 m below the first detected surface", and "marsh vegetation is typically dense enough that the first return will almost always be from the top or somewhere near the" canopy. Ussyshkin & Theriault (2011, Remote Sensing 3:416) give the minimum pulse-separation distance as 2.14 m for the ALTM 3100, 1.54 m for the ALTM Gemini, and 0.64–0.67 m for the later Orion. Hopkinson et al. (2005) state that their ALTM 2050 could not separate "returns less than 4.6 m apart. Therefore, only single (i.e., coincident first and last) returns can be recorded in canopies lower than this height". Fernandez-Nunez et al. (2017; abstract): "the dense stands of low (< 1 m) vegetation commonly found precludes the acquisition of more than one return, and the resulting DTM is not different to the DSM". Wang et al. (2009, IEEE TGRS; abstract) similarly note that short marsh vegetation "does not give rise to detectable differences between first and last LiDAR returns". The 2008 Minnesota sensors (Gemini, ALS50-2) therefore cannot resolve any herbaceous canopy from the ground within one pulse, which is consistent with a 98% single-return population.

**Where the single return sits.** The pooled evidence puts the discrete range at roughly one third of canopy height in grass-like canopies: 34% bias-to-height (Rogers et al. 2016); grass/herb penetration of 0.10 m or 33% of canopy (Hopkinson et al. 2005); Rogers et al. cite Populus et al. (2001; not retrieved) for "roughly half the canopy height". In closed sedge the lowest return in a 1 m sample sat 95 cm above ground under 1.3 m Carex obnupta (Ewald 2013), and Buffington et al. (2016) cite Sadro et al. (2007) that "just 3% of lidar points were reflected off the marsh surface". Rogers et al. (2016) found lidar error versus vegetation height r² = 0.49 overall, and Parrish, Rogers & Calder (2014, IEEE GRSL; abstract) that "a simple estimate of waveform width can explain over 50% of the total variability in elevation differences".

**Full-waveform versus discrete.** Rogers et al. (2016) built relative uncertainty surfaces from waveform width and amplitude that "correlate well with vegetation height (r = 0.85; n = 268)". A full-waveform study of French foredunes (Remote Sensing 2018, 10:669) found that marram grass (0.2–0.5 m) and couch grass (0.2–0.3 m) "display only one single FWFp and discrete echo", with marram grass showing "an upward FWF damping" relative to sand: the vegetation signal is a change in pulse shape, not a second peak. Zlinszky et al. (2014, Remote Sensing 6:8056; Riegl LMS-Q680, 1550 nm, 12.8 echoes/m²) found "Differences in the order of magnitude of 0.1 ns in mean echo width ... between mown and abandoned meadows". Anderson et al. (2016, Remote Sens. Ecol. Conserv.; abstract) found discrete-return data "more biased and less consistent" than waveform data for canopy height and understorey.

**Point density.** Buffington et al. (2016): "The higher point density of the PNW lidar dataset (8 pts/m vs. 1 pt/m) did not appear to have an effect on lidar error". Ewald (2013) at 7.2 returns/m² concluded that "more advanced filtering procedures are unlikely to improve LIDAR-based estimates of the ground surface elevation because no LIDAR information is available for the filters". Anderson et al. (2024) got better Juncus ground from the lower-density, higher-altitude flights (17 versus 49 cm RMSE). Density helps only where some pulses reach the ground.

**Intensity of senesced versus green canopies.** No lidar study was found that compares 1064 or 1550 nm return intensity of senesced and green herbaceous canopies. Elvidge (1990, Int. J. Remote Sensing; abstract) establishes that in green leaves "Chlorophyll and water obscure the absorption features of all other leaf constituents", whereas dry-plant near-IR features "originate from lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose". Zlinszky et al. (2014) found 1550 nm reflectance useful for mowing status; Šrollerů & Potůčková (2024, Int. J. Remote Sensing; abstract-level) reportedly excluded intensity because it did not differ between grass species.

## 4. Correction methods and what they achieve

**Class-specific offsets calibrated with RTK.** Hladik & Alber (2012) reduced overall mean error "from 0.10 ± 0.12 (SD) to − 0.01 ± 0.09 m (SD), and the Root Mean Square Error from 0.16 m to 0.10 m" with ten cover-class factors, three of them S. alterniflora height classes; Hladik, Schalles & Alber (2013, RSE 139:318–330; abstract) extended this with a 90%-accurate hyperspectral map, "overall mean error decreased from 0.10 ± 0.12 (SD) to − 0.003 ± 0.10 m, and root mean squared error from 0.15 to 0.10 m". Fernandez-Nunez et al. (2017; abstract) reached "average error 0.1 m" with habitat-specific factors from an 82%-accurate object-based map. Medeiros et al. (2015; abstract) with a two-class biomass scheme reduced RMSE "from 0.65 m to 0.40 m, a 38% improvement" and mean error from 0.61 to 0.32 m. All need a cover map and RTK ground truth per class.

**Vegetation-index regression.** Buffington et al. (2016) regress lidar error on lidar elevation and NAIP NDVI (LEAN): "The LEAN model reduced lidar bias by an average of 58.5% across all sites, ranging from 40-75%", final RMSE 0.072 m (SD 0.018), mean error 0; about 118 RTK points calibrated a site model to within 1 cm of cross-validated RMSE. Against alternatives on the same data: mean-error correction 0.096 m RMSE; 5 m minimum-bin gridding "increased mean RMSE across sites to 0.271 m, and ME was positively biased at 0.065 m"; vegetation-correction-factor DEMs 0.12 and 0.084 m versus LEAN 0.051 and 0.070 m. NDVI from a different year gave 0.059 versus 0.065 m, so the method is "insensitive to temporal mismatches between NDVI and lidar datasets". A 2019 Remote Sensing study (11:2346) using Landsat, NLCD and random forest reduced Fire Island error from ME 0.25 m / RMSE 0.30 m to ME 0.00 m / RMSE 0.08 m. Medeiros et al. (2022) with random forest on Sentinel-2 bands found "that local ground truth data are necessary for mitigating bias" and that models trained on one season or year do not transfer.

**Percentile and minimum-bin estimators.** Schmid et al. (2011; abstract) report that minimum-bin gridding of bare-earth points reduced "overall bias ... by 12 cm, and the vertical accuracy was improved by 8 cm", with optimal cells (per Ewald 2013) of 4.0 m in S. alterniflora and 10.0 m in Juncus roemerianus. Ewald's own optimum was 1.4 m, which cut whole-site bias to 1.6 cm (MAE 24.3 cm, RMSE 39.7 cm) but left 36–49 cm under Carex, because larger cells "capture and favor low features within the landscape, such as channels and ditches". Rogers et al. (2016) minimum bin (1 m²) cut all-vegetation bias from 0.14 to 0.09 m (σ 0.15) and S. alterniflora from 0.23 to 0.16 m; Terrascan ground filtering gave 0.11 m. Anderson et al. (2024) used a lowest-fraction "Ground Percentage Estimation" selector. Göpfert & Heipke (2006, ISPRS Archives; abstract-level) proposed correcting the "systematic positive height shift" of sub-20 cm vegetation from height-texture measures in the laser data alone. Across these, a low percentile or minimum removes part of the bias in open sward and none in closed sward.

**Full-waveform and UAV routes.** Rogers et al. (2018, Estuarine Coastal Shelf Sci. 202:193–211; abstract), regressing on waveform features, tidal-datum elevation and distance from shoreline, "estimated ground elevation with a mean bias of 0.00 m and a standard deviation of 0.07 m" without a habitat map. Pinton et al. (2020, ESPL 45:3687–3701; abstract) convert the 3D distribution of ~5 cm UAV-lidar returns into ground elevation, vegetation height and density at 40 × 40 cm, calibrated on S. alterniflora. Terrestrial scanning remains the reference for what a sward hides: Coveney & Fotheringham (2011, Photogrammetric Record; abstract-level) found vegetation occlusion a dominant TLS error term, and Luscombe et al. (2015, Ecohydrology 8:584–594; abstract) conclude that "LiDAR digital surface models are subject to specific errors related to short-sward ecosystem structure".

## 5. Management dynamics: haying, mowing, grazing, burning, flooding

The literature treats management as a nuisance for vegetation mapping, not as a term in the ground-elevation error budget. Zlinszky et al. (2014) flew leaf-on (July 2011) and leaf-off (March 2012) over Hungarian Natura 2000 meadows and record that "the grass was unfortunately mown shortly before the flight", that "in a meadow that was only partly mown at the time of flight, an NDSM height difference of 20 cm represented an approximate difference of 50–100 cm in vegetation height", and that "the leaf-off NDSM showed vehicle tracks, mowing strips and historic plowing marks". Within-season cutting therefore changes the lidar surface by decimetres on a hay meadow, and a leaf-off surface still carries the previous season's management. Su & Bork (2006) note that freshwater meadows "were typically flooded in spring and grazed by cattle during summer", leaving "numerous pits created by cattle hooves". Burning gives the cleanest before/after numbers: Tienaho et al. (2025; abstract) found TLS DTMs 8–13 cm lower after ground vegetation burned, and Nelson, Chasmer & Hopkinson (2022, Remote Sensing 14:5080) found airborne bias of 0.00 m at all post-fire stages with RMSE rising from 0.09 m (short regeneration, 0.20–0.35 m) to 0.16 m (tall regeneration, 0.40–1.00 m) and 0.19 m (unburned). Hopkinson et al. (2005) attribute their +0.15 m aquatic-class error to saturated ground; Medeiros et al. (2022) name "standing water and dense vegetation (typically tall, stiff grasses with heights greater than 1 m)" as "the primary cause of the widespread biases". For two-epoch change detection, Rogers et al. (2016) is the only study found that differences two flights, and its difference surface is dominated by vegetation growth (r² = 0.91 with lidar error); Viedma (2022, Forests 13:380; abstract) reconciles two epochs of different density with a DoD-derived "pseudo-geoid", but for forest. No study relates haying, regrowth or autumn senescence of a floodplain hay field to the surface recorded by a later lidar epoch.

## 6. Gaps

No retrieved study gives (1) a lidar ground offset for Phalaris arundinacea, which occurs only as a minor species in Ewald's (2013) quadrats; (2) the offset for senesced standing grass under a 1 pt/m², single-return, 2008-era sensor, since the dormant-season evidence (Rogers 2016; Buffington 2016; Ewald 2013) is all coastal marsh at 1–8 pts/m² with later sensors; (3) thatch or lodged-litter thickness and the offset between thatch top and mineral soil that a spring flight records; (4) hay-field cutting dates and regrowth paired with ground error; (5) 1064 nm intensity of dead versus green sward; or (6) ground bias by crop type or stubble height, even though the standards place crops in VVA.

## Gaps and what would settle them

1. **A reed-canary-grass and sedge-meadow offset table.** RTK ground elevations at 50–100 points per cover class in sedge meadow and Phalaris hay field, with concurrent canopy height, cover and thatch thickness, in a leaf-off autumn and a green-up spring: the Hladik & Alber / Ewald design on the missing species. It would give the class offsets directly and test the 34% bias-to-height ratio off the coast.
2. **Thatch-top versus mineral-soil offset after winter.** Spring thatch thickness (probe to mineral soil) alongside RTK on cut and uncut fields would quantify the surface a May flight records. No published value exists.
3. **Phenology-indexed error on hay fields.** Pair the Sentinel-2 cut/regrowth/senescence timeline with UAV-lidar or TLS before and after cutting on one field (the Zlinszky 2014 problem done deliberately), to test whether a cut field in 2021 and a senesced field in 2008 differ by the 100–250 mm observed.
4. **Single-return range position for a 2008-era sensor.** A TLS reference surface under senesced grass against Gemini/ALS50-class returns would pin where the single return falls for stiff dead stems versus the 33–35% found for live marsh grasses; the closest existing test is Hopkinson's August 0.10 m grass penetration.
5. **Intensity discrimination of dead sward.** A calibrated 1064 nm amplitude comparison of green, cut and senesced grass would settle whether intensity can flag the closed-sward cells where no estimator works.
