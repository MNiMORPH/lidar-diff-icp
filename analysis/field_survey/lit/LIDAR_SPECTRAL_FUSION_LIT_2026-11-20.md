## Findings by paper

### F01. Onojeghuo & Blackburn 2011, hyperspectral + LiDAR for reedbeds (Cumbria, UK) -- ABSTRACT LEVEL
Onojeghuo, A.O., and G.A. Blackburn. 2011. Optimising the use of hyperspectral and LiDAR data for mapping reedbed habitats. Remote Sensing of Environment 115(8): 2025-2034. doi:10.1016/j.rse.2011.04.004
Airborne hyperspectral + ALS at two Cumbrian sites; target Phragmites reedbeds. Hyperspectral dimensionality reduced by PCA / SSPCA / MNF; SSPCA best. LiDAR used as a canopy-height-model MASK (3 m threshold), which raised reedbed accuracy by about 11 % on the best SSPCA map (abstract wording). LiDAR structure here is a height gate, not a return-shape descriptor. Companion: Onojeghuo & Blackburn 2013, Characterising reedbeds using LiDAR data, IEEE JSTARS 6(2): 935-941 (not yet read). <!-- spelling: ok (verbatim title) -->
Meaning for Elba: the one canonical reedbed fusion paper used lidar only as a height mask; Phragmites at 2-4 m is far easier to gate than RCG at 0.6-2 m.

### F02. Zlinszky et al. 2012, ALS wetland vegetation categories, Lake Balaton -- FULL TEXT READ (pdftotext)
Zlinszky, A., W. Muecke, H. Lehner, C. Briese, and N. Pfeifer. 2012. Categorizing Wetland Vegetation by Airborne Laser Scanning on Lake Balaton and Kis-Balaton, Hungary. Remote Sensing 4(6): 1617-1650. doi:10.3390/rs4061617
Sensor: "A Leica ALS50 sensor operating at 1,064 nm wavelength with a sinusoidal scan pattern was employed. With this instrument a maximum of four echoes can be distinguished for each pulse ... 1 pt/m2 point density, 22 cm footprint diameter and ca. 1 km swath width from an elevation of 1,200 m above ground level." "Echo amplitudes were modulated by an automatic gain control (AGC) and the AGC and amplitude values were included in the attributes of each point." August 2010, 82 ground-truth plots (~10 x 10 m), ~8,000 m2 of reference for ~100 km2 of wetland.
Intensity handling: radiometric calibration with OPALS RadioCal against in situ ASD targets measured during the flight: "white dolomite gravel parking space, reflectance at 1,064 nm: 53.5 +/- 3.8%" and "freshly deposited bare topsoil, reflectance at 1,064 nm: 13.8 +/- 2.0%"; the tool "corrected each echo amplitude value for ... atmospheric attenuation, the range, the incidence angle and the area and reflectance of the footprint" plus the AGC, giving "calibrated reflectance values for each echo as a dimensionless number between 0 and 1", rasterized at 1 m. Known failure: AGC lag of "about one second" at water-to-land transitions oversaturated the receiver, so calibrated reflectance near shorelines is wrong.
Features (raster decision tree): calibrated reflectance, nDSM height, surface (grid) variance, dropout point count, Sigma Z, DTM variance. Decision thresholds (Table 1): Carex reflectance > 0.22 AND nDSM < 0.95 m AND variance < 0.04; Typha reflectance < 0.155 AND variance < 0.3; ruderal reed reflectance > 0.4; healthy reed < 0.4; die-back reed 0.07-0.34 with dropouts > 3; stressed reed variance > 0.2.
Signature analysis (Fig. 6): "Carex and Typha can apparently well be separated from each other based on reflectance, but not from Reed."
Accuracy (Table 2, 775 control points, 9 classes): overall 82.71 %, kappa 0.80. Producer's / user's: Typha 88.6 / 72.9; Carex 60.4 / 82.9; die-back reed 76.5 / 62.5; stressed reed 72.9 / 80.4; ruderal reed 78.6 / 84.6; healthy reed 80.7 / 80.2; tree 98.0 / 100; water 88.9 / 99.1; Scirpus 92.3 / 97.3. Merging Carex+Typha and stressed+die-back: 86.6 %, kappa 0.85. Reed presence as a whole ">90%".
Named limitation: "the limitations of the dataset (e.g., low canopy penetration)"; structural parameters (height, density, biomass) were deliberately not attempted at 1 pt/m2.
Meaning for Elba: the closest published analog to the 2008 MN DNR data (same instrument family, Leica ALS50 at 1,064 nm, 1 pt/m2, 8-bit AGC intensity). With that data, calibrated 1,064 nm reflectance separated a sedge (Carex, bright, > 0.22) from Typha (dark, < 0.155) but NOT from reed; the herbaceous classes (Carex, Typha) were the weakest (60-89 %). It required same-day in situ reflectance targets and the AGC log, neither of which exists for the 2008 MN delivery. Carex being BRIGHT at 1064 nm in August is a green-leaf NIR signal; the November 2008 flight saw senesced canopies, for which this paper has no result.

### F03. Wichmann et al. 2015, first Optech Titan flight, Oshawa, Ontario -- FULL TEXT READ (pdftotext)
Wichmann, V., M. Bremer, J. Lindenberger, M. Rutzinger, C. Georges, and F. Petrini-Monteferri. 2015. Evaluating the potential of multispectral airborne LiDAR for topographic mapping and land cover classification. ISPRS Annals II-3/W5: 113-119. doi:10.5194/isprsannals-II-3-W5-113-2015
Titan channels C1 1,550 nm, C2 1,064 nm, C3 532 nm; C1 and C3 tilted +3.5 and +7 degrees forward; 300 kHz per channel; ~5 pts/m2 per channel; channels merged by nearest neighbor within 1 m, absence of a neighbor coded as a drop-out (intensity 0). Intensity handling: NO range or incidence correction ("no detailed analysis on beam incidence angle effects ... could be conducted"); per-channel 99.99th-percentile cutoff then a histogram stretch to +/-2 SD onto 8 bits; pseudo-NDVI = (C2 - C3)/(C2 + C3).
Ground sub-classes separated by hand-drawn training + Mahalanobis pattern matching on the three channel peaks (C1, C2, C3, 8-bit scaled): "'wetlands' (~50,50,40) to 'green grass' (170,200,110), to 'dried up grass' (110,60,75) and 'sand / bare soil' (210,130,140)". "In theory, the moisture and vegetation density is increasing with channel intensity." Single-channel confusions: "In C1 'darker asphalt' shows the same peak as 'wetlands' and 'lighter asphalt' is very similar to 'dried up grass'. In C2 'lighter asphalt' shows the same peak as 'wetlands' and 'dried up grass'."
Accuracy reported only for sealed vs unsealed ground (user's 0.95 / producer's 0.94 for unsealed; 0.74 / 0.77 for sealed); NO accuracy for green vs dried grass. Multi-return caveat: "points with higher return numbers ... contain underestimated reflectance values, they tend to be grouped to classes with low reflectance values such as 'wetlands'"; "the false classification of multi echoes from 'green grass' into the rarely reflecting 'wetlands' class."
Meaning for Elba: the one published number pair for green vs dried grass at airborne lidar wavelengths: at 1,064 nm green grass ~200 vs dried-up grass ~60 (8-bit stretched, uncalibrated, September leaf-on, Ontario); at 1,550 nm 170 vs 110; at 532 nm 110 vs 75. On this single flight DRIED grass was DARKER than green grass in all three channels, strongest at 1,064 nm. The values are stretched histogram peaks, not reflectances, and were not validated against field data.

### F04. Huo et al. 2018, Titan urban classification, Oshawa -- READ (PMC full text via fetch)
Huo, L.-Z., C.A. Silva, C. Klauberg, M. Mohan, L.-J. Zhao, P. Tang, and A.T. Hudak. 2018. Supervised spatial classification of multispectral LiDAR data in urban areas. PLoS ONE 13(10): e0206185. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0206185
Titan 1,550 / 1,064 / 532 nm, 3 km2 strip, 3 September 2014, leaf-on. Classes road, building, tree, grass (no low-veg or wetland class). Intensity normalized by (R_i/R_ref)^2 with R_ref 1,075 m (range only). Intensity-only OA 74.66 % (kappa 0.54); best model (mean intensity + pseudoNDVI + nDSM + hierarchical morphological profiles) OA 93.28 %, kappa 0.91; grass producer's 85.71 -> 95.51 %.
Meaning for Elba: Titan intensity alone gives ~75 % on four coarse urban classes; grass is one class, never typed.

### F05. Ekhtari, Glennie & Fernandez-Diaz 2018, Titan point-cloud land cover -- ABSTRACT LEVEL (bibliographic record; Pan et al. 2020 ISPRS citing)
Ekhtari, N., C. Glennie, and J.C. Fernandez-Diaz. 2018. Classification of airborne multispectral lidar point clouds for land cover mapping. IEEE JSTARS 11(6): 2068-2078. doi:10.1109/JSTARS.2018.2835483 (DOI from memory of the series; UNVERIFIED this session)
Ten land-cover classes (grass, trees, soil, pavement, buildings, ...) from the three Titan intensities plus spatial metrics; SVM on SINGLE-return points OA 94.7 %, kappa 0.94; a second approach including multiple returns 79.7 %. Grass is a single class.
Meaning for Elba: the single-return restriction is the same physics as Wichmann's multi-echo caveat: intensity is only a spectral measurement when the whole pulse hits one target. In a sward where 96-99 % of 2021 returns are single, that condition holds, which is a point in favor of using 2021 intensity.

### F06. Hopkinson et al. 2016, three-wavelength forest, Canada -- ABSTRACT LEVEL
Hopkinson, C., L. Chasmer, C. Gynan, C. Mahoney, and M. Sitar. 2016. Multisensor and multispectral LiDAR characterization and classification of a forest environment. Canadian Journal of Remote Sensing 42(5): 501-520. doi:10.1080/07038992.2016.1196584 (open access, hdl.handle.net/10133/7401)
2013 three independent sensors (Gemini 1,064; Aquarius 532; Orion 1,550) vs 2015 Titan. "Canopy attenuation was greatest at 532 nm, presumably due to leaf tissue absorption." "Relative to 1064 nm, foliage was undersampled at midheight percentiles at 1550 nm and 532 nm." Single-sensor data showed "clear wavelength-dependent variation that could be exploited in intensity-based land cover classification without the aid of 3D derivatives." Describes 1,064 nm as the most commonly adopted commercial wavelength.
Meaning for Elba: the three channels have different penetration as well as different reflectance, so a multispectral lidar return-height distribution is itself wavelength-dependent; with both Elba epochs at 1,064 nm, the shape difference between epochs is not a wavelength effect.

### F07. Chust et al. 2008, Bidasoa estuary, ALS height + intensity + 4-band imagery -- ABSTRACT LEVEL
Chust, G., I. Galparsoro, A. Borja, J. Franco, and A. Uriarte. 2008. Coastal and estuarine habitat mapping, using LIDAR height and intensity and multi-spectral imagery. Estuarine, Coastal and Shelf Science 78(4): 633-643. doi:10.1016/j.ecss.2008.02.003
Maximum likelihood on visible + NIR imagery, LIDAR DTM, slope, aspect, intensity. The DTM helped most (+10 to +27 % agreement); "Raw LIDAR intensity images are only of limited value" because speckled; an enhanced Lee filter on intensity gave up to +7.9 % producer's / +11.6 % user's in the wetland zone. Final mean producer's accuracy 92 % (16 rocky habitats), 88 % (11 wetland habitats, salt-marsh plant communities included).
Meaning for Elba: intensity contributed after smoothing, i.e. as a per-patch not per-point measurement; elevation (microtopography) did the heavy lifting for marsh communities, which is a floodplain-relevant point: hydroperiod, read through elevation, predicts community.

### F08. Rapinel, Hubert-Moy & Clement 2015, ALS + multi-season multispectral wetland habitats, France -- ABSTRACT LEVEL (HAL record; full text blocked)
Rapinel, S., L. Hubert-Moy, and B. Clement. 2015. Combined use of LiDAR data and multispectral earth observation imagery for wetland habitat mapping. International Journal of Applied Earth Observation and Geoinformation 37: 56-64. doi:10.1016/j.jag.2014.09.002 (VERIFIED via Crossref; HAL id hal-01098372)
Object-based decision tree on multi-season multispectral images plus three LiDAR layers: vegetation height, microtopography, return intensity. Overall accuracy 86.5 % combined; height and microtopography credited with most of the gain; intensity's contribution not quantified in the abstract.
Meaning for Elba: this is the template Andy describes (lidar structure + seasonal spectra), but published at habitat level (reed bed, wet meadow, etc.), not species, and the lidar side was height and microtopography, not return-distribution shape.

### F09. Bourgeau-Chavez et al. 2013 / 2015, Great Lakes Phragmites, PALSAR + Landsat -- ABSTRACT LEVEL
Bourgeau-Chavez, L.L., K.P. Kowalski, M.L. Carlson Mazur, K.A. Scarbrough, R.B. Powell, C.N. Brooks, B. Huberty, L.K. Jenkins, E.C. Banda, D.M. Galbraith, Z.M. Laubach, and K. Riordan. 2013. Mapping invasive Phragmites australis in the coastal Great Lakes with ALOS PALSAR satellite imagery for decision support. Journal of Great Lakes Research 39(S1): 65-77. doi:10.1016/j.jglr.2012.11.001 (issue/pages UNVERIFIED)
Multi-season L-band PALSAR; basin-wide dense Phragmites map; overall 87 %, Phragmites producer's 86 %, user's 43 %. Bourgeau-Chavez et al. 2015, Remote Sensing 7(7): 8655-8682 (doi:10.3390/rs70708655, UNVERIFIED): three-season Landsat TM + PALSAR coastal wetland map, overall 94 %. Lidar appears in the USGS decision-support tool only as topo-bathymetry for lake-level scenarios, not in the classifier.
Meaning for Elba: the Great Lakes invasive-reed program never used lidar structure or intensity for detection; its signal was L-band volume scattering from tall dense dead-and-live reed, which RCG at 1-2 m would give weakly.

### F10. Marcinkowska-Ochtyra et al. 2018, expansive grasses, hyperspectral + ALS, Poland -- ABSTRACT READ (Crossref)
Marcinkowska-Ochtyra, A., A. Jarocinska, K. Bzdega, and B. Tokarska-Guzik. 2018. Classification of expansive grassland species in different growth stages based on hyperspectral and LiDAR data. Remote Sensing 10(12): 2019. doi:10.3390/rs10122019
HySpex 1 m hyperspectral + ALS (discrete and full-waveform), Random Forest, Molinia caerulea and Calamagrostis epigejos in a Natura 2000 area, late spring to early autumn. "For M. caerulea the highest median Kappa was 0.85 (F1 = 0.89) in August" "and for C. epigejos 0.65 (F1 = 0.73) in September." Adding LiDAR improved both.
Meaning for Elba: the closest published case to "an expansive grass vs the rest of a meadow" with lidar in the stack; the best date was species-specific (August vs September) and the harder grass reached only kappa 0.65 even with 1 m hyperspectral. Which lidar variables helped is in the body, not the abstract (not read).

### F11. Alexander et al. 2015, full-waveform ALS classification of open alkali landscape, Hungary -- ABSTRACT LEVEL (search snippet; ADS page returned empty)
Alexander, C., B. Deak, and H. Heilmeier. 2015. Classification of vegetation in an open landscape using full-waveform airborne laser scanner data. International Journal of Applied Earth Observation and Geoinformation 41: 76-87. doi:10.1016/j.jag.2015.04.014 (pages/DOI UNVERIFIED)
Pannonic salt steppes/marshes and loess steppic grasslands; waveform attributes (echo width, amplitude) plus three DTM-derived variables from a leaf-off ALS; concludes that "structural and spectral attributes of ALS echoes" can classify open landscapes "especially where elevation shapes vegetation." Accuracy numbers not captured.

### F12. Kashani, Olsen, Parrish & Wilson 2015, review of lidar radiometric processing -- ABSTRACT/TABLE LEVEL
Kashani, A.G., M.J. Olsen, C.E. Parrish, and N. Wilson. 2015. A review of LIDAR radiometric processing: from ad hoc intensity correction to rigorous radiometric calibration. Sensors 15(11): 28099-28128. doi:10.3390/s151128099
Controls on recorded intensity: range (1/R^2 for extended targets), incidence angle (cosine-type fall-off), atmospheric transmission, footprint area and target reflectance, plus instrument factors: transmitted power, receiver gain and especially AGC, which "can produce discontinuities in the intensity signal if not compensated". Terminology: "correction" = removing range/angle effects; "normalization" = relative scaling between strips; "rigorous calibration" = conversion to reflectance via known targets. Standard range correction I_c = I (R/R_ref)^2 (as used by Huo et al. 2018); Zlinszky 2012 used the full OPALS physical model with targets.
Meaning for Elba: the 2008 delivery (AGC on, 8-bit, no AGC log published) cannot be rigorously calibrated; it can at most be range-corrected and compared RELATIVELY within one flight line. The 2021 TerrainMapper delivery has no published gain log either; range and scan angle are recoverable from the trajectory and the per-point scan angle rank, so a range + incidence correction is feasible for both.

### F13. Rapinel et al. 2019, Sentinel-2 time series for floodplain grassland plant communities -- ABSTRACT LEVEL (HAL record)
Rapinel, S., C. Mony, L. Lecoq, B. Clement, A. Thomas, and L. Hubert-Moy. 2019. Evaluation of Sentinel-2 time-series for mapping floodplain grassland plant communities. Remote Sensing of Environment 223: 115-129. doi:10.1016/j.rse.2019.01.018 (author list after first author and DOI UNVERIFIED)

### F14. Dual-wavelength moisture indices (Gaulton et al. 2013; Hancock, Gaulton & Danson; Li et al. 2017 DWEL) -- ABSTRACT LEVEL
Gaulton, R., F.M. Danson, F.A. Ramirez, and O. Gunawan. 2013. The potential of dual-wavelength laser scanning for estimating vegetation moisture content. Remote Sensing of Environment 132: 32-39. doi:10.1016/j.rse.2013.01.001 (volume/DOI UNVERIFIED)

### F15. Zlinszky et al. 2014, full-waveform ALS grassland categories, Sopron, Hungary -- ABSTRACT (Semantic Scholar) + repo notes

## Synthesis

Andy's question: could a return-distribution SHAPE signature be combined with wavelength information (lidar intensity at the laser wavelength, multispectral lidar, Sentinel-2 / NAIP bands) to tell herbaceous vegetation types apart, and has anyone done it?

**Has anyone done it.** In pieces, yes; as a whole, no. (1) Lidar + passive spectra for wetland and marsh habitat is an established recipe (Chust 2008, 88 % over 11 wetland habitats; Rapinel 2015, 86.5 %; Onojeghuo & Blackburn 2011, +11 % from a 3 m height mask; Gilmore 2008 in the phenology notes), but in every case the lidar term is canopy HEIGHT or microtopography, and the targets are habitats or tall emergents (Phragmites 2-4 m), not grasses of 0.6-2 m. (2) Lidar ALONE has typed herbaceous vegetation twice: Zlinszky 2012 (1,064 nm Leica ALS50, 1 pt/m2, calibrated reflectance + height + variance + dropouts; Carex 60/83 %, Typha 89/73 %, kappa 0.80 over 9 classes) and Zlinszky 2014 (1,550 nm full waveform, 12.8/m2, two seasons; 68-75 %). (3) Multispectral lidar (Titan 532/1,064/1,550) separates green grass from dried grass by intensity pattern on one September flight (Wichmann 2015: 1,064 nm peaks ~200 vs ~60, 8-bit stretched, no accuracy given) and reaches 90-95 % on coarse urban classes where "grass" is one class (Morsy 2017; Huo 2018; Ekhtari 2018, 94.7 % on single returns); no Titan study of wetland, marsh or grassland TYPES was found. (4) Hyperspectral + ALS has separated two expansive grasses from a meadow matrix at kappa 0.85 and 0.65 (Marcinkowska-Ochtyra 2018), the closest analog to RCG-vs-meadow. (5) Nothing found combines a discrete-return HEIGHT-DISTRIBUTION SHAPE (peak height, width, ground share) with Sentinel-2 phenology or NAIP for herbaceous type; the 2019-2025 ALS + Sentinel-2 fusion papers are forest (Njimi 2024) or fuel type. (6) No reed canary grass study with lidar, hyperspectral, UAV or Sentinel-2 exists as far as three searches (two extended) can tell; the Wisconsin Landsat maps remain the only RCG remote-sensing product.

**What the two sensors' wavelengths imply.** Every instrument in both Elba epochs is a single-channel 1,064 nm lidar (TerrainMapper 90515: VERIFIED brochure; ALS50-II and Gemini: manufacturer/NCALM records). So there is no within-lidar spectral ratio, and 1,064 nm is the worst single wavelength for the green-vs-dead question: the dry/green contrast lives in the SWIR water absorption (Gaulton 2013 R2 0.8 at 1,063/1,545 nm; NPV literature), while at 1,064 nm both green and senesced grass sit on the NIR plateau. Wichmann's one data point says dried grass was darker at 1,064 nm than green grass in September; Garroway 2011 says vegetation attenuation and flight-line edge, not surface state, dominated 1,064 nm intensity over a 60 cm sward. The 2008 intensities are 8-bit under AGC with no gain log (same family as Zlinszky 2012, who needed same-day targets); 2021 is digitized at 14 bits by a sensor whose gain handling is undocumented. Intensity from these data is therefore a RELATIVE, per-flight-line, scan-angle-conditioned covariate, never a reflectance, and it cannot be compared across the two epochs.

**What is usable.** The strongest supported route is the one nobody has published: use the 2021 return-height shape metrics (and 2021 single-return intensity, range- and scan-angle-corrected within line 42/43) as STRUCTURE covariates, and let Sentinel-2 / NAIP phenology carry the spectral discrimination, with the field RTK points as truth. The literature ceiling for the spectral half alone is ~78 % for seven wet-grassland communities with a full-year Sentinel-2 stack (Rapinel 2019). The 2008 data contribute a leaf-off standing-dead height (level of the 0.2-0.4 m population) that no paper has measured for RCG; the Nordic delayed-harvest literature and Minnesota Wildflowers say the canopy stands through winter, which is why that level varies by place.

**Not in the literature.** A 1,064 nm intensity comparison of green vs standing-dead herbaceous canopies; sward-density vs ground-return fraction for any grass (only forest gap-fraction work and qualitative statements that dense swards block pulses; Hopkinson 2005 gives 0.10 m / 33 % penetration for grass-herb but no return fraction); multispectral lidar on herbaceous wetland types; RCG with any active sensor; and any paper fusing discrete-return height-distribution SHAPE with satellite phenology for herbaceous classification.
