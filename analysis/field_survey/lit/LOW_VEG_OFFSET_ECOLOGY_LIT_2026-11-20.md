## Question

At the Elba floodplain (Whitewater River, MN; hay meadow and nettle/grass/goldenrod ground), every return of the
May 2021 3DEP flight (1,064 nm, ~17 pulses/m^2) sits 0.13 m above RTK ground with a 3-4 cm spread; 1-4 % of
pulses reach the soil; the vendor class-2 "ground" is that sward surface; the November 2008 flight (~1 pulse/m^2)
sat 0.2-0.4 m up. Available: per-point return-height distributions, per-1 m-cell structure metrics, intensity,
Sentinel-2/NAIP phenology. Andy asks: who has used airborne lidar returning from LOW vegetation (grassland, hay
meadow, marsh, crop, tundra, heath, floodplain herbs) for ECOLOGICAL purposes, deriving height, biomass, density,
cover type or condition from the offset above true ground, from penetration / ground-return fraction, from
intensity, or from the vertical return distribution, at airborne densities (0.5-30 pts/m^2)? Who has mapped
condition or phenology from the lidar-minus-ground offset, or used leaf-off vs leaf-on repeat lidar of herbaceous
cover?

### A. Salt marsh: the offset read as an ecological signal (height, biomass, invasion, condition)

**Rosso, P.H., Ustin, S.L., Hastings, A. (2006). Use of lidar to study changes associated with Spartina invasion in San Francisco Bay marshes. Remote Sensing of Environment 100(3), 295-306. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2005.10.012** [ABS-paraphrase via manuscript.isc.ac record; Crossref-verified; full text not reached: escholarship, ResearchGate, infona all failed] Keywords: Lidar mapping; Change detection; Tidal salt marshes; Relative accuracy; Spartina invasion. The record's abstract paraphrase: the study tested whether airborne lidar can measure topography and vegetation structure of SF Bay tidal marshes; found measurable errors between flight lines and among returns; ground comparisons showed lidar "did not penetrate the vegetation to reach the ground"; nonetheless repeat-DSM differencing showed Spartina patches expanding at up to ~2.5 m/yr and altering mudflat erosion, accretion and drainage; the authors concluded lidar could help distinguish Spartina species and marsh features and track sediment change and biomass. Secondary sources (search level, UNVERIFIED) put the vertical error at 20-30 cm in Spartina. Point density, flight dates and whether ground came from survey: not captured. Relevance: the first paper to turn the "lidar does not reach the ground" problem into a MONITORING signal, by differencing two surface epochs of a marsh so that the vegetated-surface change (invasion front, canopy growth) is the quantity measured, exactly the inversion Andy is contemplating for the Elba DoD; but it is two surface models of the same dense canopy type, not a leaf-off/green-up pair, and it reports relative accuracy rather than offset-vs-biomass.

**Rogers, J.N., Parrish, C.E., Ward, L.G., Burdick, D.M. (2015). Evaluation of field-measured vertical obscuration and full waveform lidar to assess salt marsh vegetation biophysical parameters. Remote Sensing of Environment 156, 264-275. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2014.09.035** [ABS, UNH scholars record] Defines "vertical obscuration" as "the measure of the vertical distribution of the ratio of vegetation to airspace", measured in the field with a new method; r > 0.9 between subsample vertical biomass and vertical obscuration for Spartina alterniflora; full-waveform metrics (waveform width) "offer new information about marsh vegetation"; Rogers 2018 (below) adds that waveform amplitude and waveform SD "were found to account for nearly 75% of the variability in vegetation height (Rogers et al., 2015)". Relevance: the one marsh paper that treats the lidar pulse's interaction with the sward as a plant-structure measurement (a profile of occlusion with height) rather than an error; it needed full waveform, and its vegetation descriptor is a within-pulse shape, which neither Elba epoch has.

**Rogers, J.N., Parrish, C.E., Ward, L.G., Burdick, D.M. (2018). Improving salt marsh digital elevation model accuracy with full-waveform lidar and nonparametric predictive modeling. Estuarine, Coastal and Shelf Science 202, 193-211. DOI 10.1016/j.ecss.2017.11.034** [FULL; NOAA repository accepted manuscript; repo previously held the abstract only] NCALM Optech Gemini + 8-bit IWD waveform digitizer, 20 July 2010 at low tide "during peak biomass", 70 kHz, 600 m AGL, ~5 pts/m2, 0.25 mrad; transmit pulse ~12 ns FWHM "corresponding to ~1.8 m of range", so "Salt marsh vegetation with heights significantly less than the range-equivalent transmit pulse width typically show return waveforms that contain just a single peak" and the dataset "was almost entirely composed of single return pulses". Ground truth: 694 vegetated RTK GNSS points (785 with species/ground type), Trimble R8, RMSE < 1 cm horizontal / 2 cm vertical. Uncorrected bias over all vegetation +0.24 m (SD 0.23, RMSE 0.33); S. alterniflora +0.35 m (SD 0.22, RMSE 0.41); S. patens, D. spicata, Salicornia +0.05 to +0.06 m (SD 0.05-0.08). TreeNet model on waveform width, uncorrected lidar elevation, distance from shoreline, amplitude, slope: test R2 0.96, bias 0.00 m, SD 0.07 m; discrete-return-only models R2 0.92, bias -0.01, SD 0.14 m, with the shorter species then UNDER-estimated by 0.07-0.08 m. Ecological content: "as marsh elevation decreases the vegetation height increases" is used as a predictor (distance from shoreline second most important after waveform width); a high-marsh/low-marsh zone map is a by-product. The paper closes by proposing a winter (senescent) re-flight "to determine if this technique is adaptable to data collected at different times of the year". Relevance: the best-measured single-return sward dataset in print; its waveform width is the within-pulse analog of the between-return spread Andy measures; the explicit statement that vegetation shorter than the range-equivalent pulse width yields one peak is the physical reason the 2008 Gemini/ALS50 data (same sensor family) show a single population, and the 2018 recommendation of a senescent re-flight has, as far as this search found, not been carried out by that group.

**Kulawardhana, R.W., Popescu, S.C., Feagin, R.A. (2014). Fusion of lidar and multispectral data to quantify salt marsh carbon stocks. Remote Sensing of Environment 154, 345-357. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2013.10.036** [CITED-IN / poster; Crossref-verified; abstract not retrievable from Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar or search] Galveston Island, Texas, S. alterniflora-dominated. The group's NASA CCE 2015 poster (Feagin et al.) states: "We combined LIDAR and multi-spectral imagery to predict field aboveground biomass in herbaceous marshes"; "Our best LIDAR model predicted ~19% off field-measured live biomass at 1 m2 resolution" and "our best LIDAR+MS models were ~16% off" (poster wording; UNVERIFIED against the paper). Point density, lidar metrics and height range not captured. Relevance: the one airborne-lidar biomass model for a herbaceous marsh at plot (1 m2) scale; the lidar metrics are presumably height/intensity (the group's forest heritage) but this could not be confirmed.

**Paynter, I., Schaaf, C., Bowen, J.L., Deegan, L., Peri, F., Cook, B. (2019). Characterizing a New England saltmarsh with NASA G-LiHT airborne lidar. Remote Sensing 11(5), 509. DOI 10.3390/rs11050509** [FULL; NSF PAR PDF] Plum Island LTER, G-LiHT Riegl VQ-480 at 1550 nm, 335 m AGL, 0.3 mrad (10 cm footprint), ranging accuracy 25 mm; June 2014, 2015, 2016 at 16.9, 14.1 and 15.3 pulses/m2 (SD ~6; up to 73 in overlaps). Classification on 1 m cells from the min/max return height per cell and mean 1550 nm intensity; the vertical datum is "a datum of marsh platform height, derived from the mean height of returns occurring in the most-occupied 0.05 m height bin", i.e. the MODE of the return-height histogram is taken as the vegetated platform. Thresholds set from TLS and field; "The marsh platform, for example, occupies only a 0.4 m height range across most of its extent"; wrack DN >= 50,000 and algae DN <= 28,000 on intensity. Phragmites extent 8,374 -> 8,882 -> 13,819 m2 over 2014-2016 (+6.1 %, +55.6 %), TLS confirms the increase but says airborne still underestimates extent; S. alterniflora extent within 7 % of TLS, but its VOLUME from airborne heights (693.3 m3 at 0.75 m) was 83.9 % above the TLS value (373.2 m3), the airborne height being too high at creek edges; the dormant-season (April 2015) TLS surface served as the ground baseline. Algae needs |scan| <= 15 deg, creeks <= 10 deg. Relevance: the one repeat-airborne-lidar marsh series at Elba-like density (14-17 pulses/m2) that maps an invasive grass and a cordgrass by height class and intensity; it uses the histogram mode as the reference level, which is the Elba 2021 "0.13 m peak" treated as datum, and it finds the airborne sward height biased HIGH relative to TLS, the opposite sign to the usual "lidar underestimates grass height", because the baseline here is a dormant-season surface. True ground came from TLS in the dormant season, not from GNSS.

**Alizad, K., Hagen, S.C., Morris, J.T., Medeiros, S.C., Bilskie, M.V., Weishampel, J.F. (2016). Coastal wetland response to sea-level rise in a fluvial estuarine system. Earth's Future 4(11), 483-497. DOI 10.1002/2016EF000385** [ABS via OpenAlex; open access] Hydro-MEM (ADCIRC + Marsh Equilibrium Model) for Apalachicola; "In order to increase accuracy, the lidar-based marsh platform topography was adjusted using Real Time Kinematic survey data"; biomass density output "validated with satellite imagery". **Alizad, K., Medeiros, S.C., Foster-Martinez, M.R., Hagen, S.C. (2020). Model sensitivity to topographic uncertainty in meso- and microtidal marshes. IEEE JSTARS 13, 807-814. DOI 10.1109/JSTARS.2020.2973490** (Crossref-verified) [FULL; NOAA repository 28831] Lidar bias "largely due to the following four factors": < 0.15 m instrument/processing error; the dead zone; standing water; small-scale biomass heterogeneity; pooled literature mean error 18 cm, SD 14 cm. RTK error (DEM minus RTK): Apalachicola -60 to +80 cm, mean +20 cm; Grand Bay -60 to +160 cm, mean +61 cm; a mean error of 30 cm is "84% of the tide range (35.8 cm)" at Grand Bay; Plum Island (tide range 2.67 m) max error 29 cm, mean 15 cm. Cohen's kappa of modeled marsh coverage vs NWI: lidar-only DEM 0.1 at both microtidal sites, RTK-adjusted 0.60 and 0.77; Plum Island 0.73 without adjustment. The adjustment at Apalachicola was spatially graded, lowering the DEM "by approximately 30 cm at the downstream end (where vegetation is generally denser and taller) and decreasing the adjustment linearly moving upriver", i.e. the correction itself encodes the biomass gradient. Relevance: the clearest statement of WHEN the sward offset matters ecologically (when it is comparable to the vertical niche width, here the tide range); the Elba analog is that a 0.13-0.40 m offset is comparable to the floodplain's inundation-depth zonation.

**Holmquist, J.R., Schile-Beers, L., Buffington, K., Lu, M., Mozdzer, T.J., Riera, J., Weller, D.E., Williams, M., Megonigal, J.P. (2021). Scalability and performance tradeoffs in quantifying relationships between elevation and tidal wetland plant communities. Marine Ecology Progress Series 666, 57-72. DOI 10.3354/meps13683** [FULL; NSF PAR PDF] Global Change Research Wetland (Chesapeake), March 2011 county lidar (1 m DEM), RTK (Trimble R8, nominal 0.04 m, repeat SD 0.006 m) on a 20 x 20 m grid plus 37 TMON plots. Four unbiased DEMs compared: single-offset subtraction (Holmquist et al. 2018 national method), LEAN, community-specific offsets, and kriging of RTK only; "more data-intensive methods such as kriging performing better than 3 more scalable methods"; LEAN "provided a compromise between scalability and performance, although it underpredicted variability in elevation". Species niches from the unbiased surface: Schoenoplectus americanus median 0.22 m NAVD88 (95 % range 0.09-0.31), Spartina patens 0.27 (0.10-0.35), Phragmites 0.19 (-0.05-0.36), Iva frutescens 0.20 (0.04-0.30). Relevance: a vegetation-niche study that is only possible once the sward offset is removed, and that documents LEAN compressing the elevation variance (the correction removes real microtopography along with the bias), a warning for any NDVI-regression correction at Elba.

### B. Floodplain and riparian herbaceous vegetation for hydraulic roughness (height AND density from the return distribution)

**Straatsma, M. (2006). Floodplain roughness mapping synergy: lidar and spectral remote sensing. ISPRS Archives XXXVI part 7 (Enschede mid-term symposium), paper 182.** [FULL; isprs.org PDF] and **Straatsma, M.W., Baptist, M.J. (2008). Floodplain roughness parameterization using airborne laser scanning and spectral remote sensing. Remote Sensing of Environment 112(3), 1062-1080. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2007.07.012** [CITED-IN / Crossref-verified; full text and abstract not retrievable (ScienceDirect, TU Delft, UU dspace 403; VLIZ PDFs were other thesis chapters); the ISPRS paper is its conference precursor with the same data and equations]. Gamerense Waard, river Waal; FLI-MAP II helicopter ALS, 11 March 2003 (leaf-off, herbs in senescence), 80 m AGL, 75 points/m2, with CASI multispectral (1700 m). Two-stage method: object-based classification into water, sand, meadow, herbaceous vegetation (incl. dry herbaceous), forest, built-up (overall accuracy 81 %, producer's accuracy for herbs 0.67-0.69); then per-class structure. For herbaceous vegetation, the verbatim key passage: "The key issue in vegetation structure mapping of non-woody herbaceous vegetation is point labelling as ground or vegetation, since the thin stalks of herbs in senescence are difficult to detect by ALS ... They modelled the histogram of the residual vertical distance between the point height and the DTM. This point height distribution is considered as the sum of the noise distribution of ground points and a uniform distribution of the vegetation points. The point of maximum upward concavity, the inflection point, is used as a threshold for labelling." This labeling is done per image object because "The height of the inflection point changes due to differences in noise levels, or flight strips that do not match exactly." Then Hv = 1.47 D95 + 0.28 (R2 0.78, N 31) and Dv = 1.18 PI + 0.03 (R2 0.66, N 43), "valid for vegetation heights in the order of 0.3 to 2 m and vegetation densities in the order of 3e-4 to 0.7 m2/m3"; the PI (vegetation points / all points) is the complement of the ground-return fraction. Meadow (short grass) is NOT given a lidar height: it is assigned a look-up roughness, i.e. below ~0.3 m the method gives up on ALS structure. ## Synthesis

**What has been done.** The literature that uses the low-vegetation lidar return as an ecological signal splits by what it treats as the datum. (1) Where survey ground exists, the offset is modeled as a function of the plant canopy and then REMOVED: Hladik & Alber 2012 (species classes), Medeiros 2015 (error = 0.0323 ln biomass, R2 0.83, from RTK), Buffington 2016 LEAN (NDVI), Rogers 2018 (waveform width, R2 0.96, bias 0.00 m, SD 0.07 m), Alizad 2016/2020 (RTK-graded correction, kappa 0.1 -> 0.6-0.77 when the offset is comparable to the tidal range), Holmquist 2021 (niche widths of 0.1-0.4 m resolved only after correction). In this strand the ecology lives in the corrected surface, and the offset is a nuisance whose magnitude is nevertheless the best-measured biomass-dependent lidar quantity in print (0.05-0.06 m for 0.2-0.5 m high-marsh species, 0.35 m for S. alterniflora at ~5 pts/m2). (2) Where no survey ground exists, the vegetated surface itself is the measurement: Rosso 2006 differenced two lidar surfaces to map Spartina invasion fronts (~2.5 m/yr) and sediment change; Paynter 2019 took the MODE of the return-height histogram as the marsh-platform datum and classified Phragmites, tall Spartina, wrack and algae by height class and 1550 nm intensity at 14-17 pulses/m2, finding airborne Spartina volume 84 % above TLS; Anderson 2010 read bog microforms from surface variance; Kulawardhana 2014 predicted marsh biomass "~19 % off" from lidar alone. (3) Where the sward is tall enough (0.3-2 m) and the density high enough (75 pts/m2), the return histogram is decomposed into ground noise plus a UNIFORM vegetation layer by its inflection point and gives height (D95, R2 0.78) and density (percentage index, R2 0.66) for hydraulic roughness (Straatsma 2006 / Straatsma & Baptist 2008; Mason 2003 for the SD-law heights); maize gives the same two quantities as CV of detrended heights and a Beer-Lambert LAI from the ground fraction that saturates at high LAI (Nie 2016; Li 2015). (4) Low woody canopies at 30 pts/m2 are read as lowest-to-highest return volume (Greaves 2016, R2 0.62); grass at 100 pts/m2 as maximum height plus cover (Zhang 2021, R2 0.54, height sliding 1 cm per 70 m of altitude); and the Boise shrub-steppe work states the herbaceous layer's biomass is NOT recoverable from airborne lidar structure even where shrub biomass is (Li 2017). The founding rangeland papers had already set the floor: canopy cover is overestimated below 0.3 m (Weltz 1994), and the Danish national product warns that its 0-0.5 m vegetation proportion "is likely biased towards an underrepresentation" because vegetation and ground cannot be separated at 4-5 pts/m2 (Assmann 2022).

**What the offset-as-signal literature achieved.** Per-species and per-biomass offset laws with RTK truth in salt marshes (the 34 % bias-to-height ratio; 0.03 m per e-fold of biomass); correction to 7-10 cm RMSE; invasion and expansion mapping of 2-4 m reeds and 1-2 m cordgrass at airborne density; height and density of 0.3-2 m senescent floodplain herbs at helicopter density; and the physical reason for the single population (vegetation shorter than the range-equivalent pulse width, ~1.8 m for the Gemini, returns one peak; Rogers 2018).

**What has NOT been done.** No paper treats a hay meadow, reed canary grass, or a mixed nettle-grass-goldenrod floodplain sward as the lidar target; the nearest are Straatsma's Rhine herbs (sedge, thistle, clover, grass, 0.26-1.66 m, March) and Zlinszky's Hungarian hay meadows (management classes, full waveform, 1550 nm). No paper uses the sward offset above RTK ground as a map of vegetation CONDITION (green vs senescent, standing vs lodged, cut vs uncut) on terrestrial grassland; the only condition reads are marsh seasonal pairs (Rogers 2016, same canopy type, 0.14 -> 0.04 m) and Zlinszky 2015's radiometric litter index. No leaf-off / green-up airborne pair over herbaceous ground has been differenced with RTK truth at both epochs; Rogers 2018 proposed the senescent re-flight and it does not appear to have been published, Paynter 2019 used a dormant-season TLS surface as the baseline, and the UAV photogrammetry literature (February + November imagery) is the only two-season low-vegetation height profile found. No paper inverts a 1 pt/m2, single-return, leaf-off dataset for standing dead sward height; Medeiros's result that the lidar's own canopy height carries R2 0.18 of biomass at 4 pts/m2, with a negative coefficient, is the closest statement of what such an inversion would yield. The Elba dataset (every 2021 return 0.13 m above RTK ground with 3-4 cm spread and 1-4 % ground returns; 2008 returns 0.2-0.4 m up and varying by place; both epochs 1064 nm single-return) therefore sits in a gap on three axes at once: the vegetation type, the two-season design with survey truth, and the use of the mode height itself as the ecological variable.
