# Return-height distribution shape as a fingerprint of herbaceous vegetation: literature (2026-11-20)

Agent literature search for Andy Wickert, lidar-diff-icp field-survey strand. Provenance tags:

## Question

At the Elba floodplain field (Whitewater River, MN), RTK ground points were compared with every
airborne lidar return within 1 m from 3DEP May 2021 (~17 returns/m^2, green-up sward) and MN DNR
November 2008 (~1 return/m^2, leaf-off). The 2021 returns form one narrow peak 0.13 m above the
ground (NMAD 3-4 cm) with a thin shelf of ground returns (1-4 % within 5 cm of the ground) and
almost no spatial variation; the 2008 returns are a broad population 0.2-0.4 m up whose level
varies by place (lowest at a tree line, highest in a wet autumn-green swale). Andy asks:

1. Is there a "full shape factor": a complete descriptor of the return-height distribution shape
   that could fingerprint herbaceous vegetation type or structure?
2. Has anyone done this before for short herbaceous / wetland / grassland / invasive-grass
   vegetation (reed canary grass, Phragmites, sedge meadow) from discrete-return airborne lidar
   height distributions, penetration ratios, intensity, echo ratios or pseudo-waveforms?

Already in the repository (not repeated at length here): `analysis/LIT_LOW_VEGETATION_LIDAR.md`
(Hopkinson 2005, Rogers 2016, Ewald 2013, Zlinszky 2014 echo width, dead-zone mechanism),
`analysis/GROUND_RETURN_PDF_LITERATURE.md` (Wagner 2006, Mallet & Bretar 2009, Mallet 2011 echo
families, Weibull/Gamma profile precedent), `analysis/ridgelines/RETURN_HEIGHT_DISTRIBUTION_LITERATURE.md`
and `analysis/ridgelines/BARE_GROUND_SKEW_LITERATURE.md` (MacArthur-Horn, Beer-Lambert,
Laplace/power-law tails), `analysis/LIT_DENSITY_GROUND_DETECTION.md` (order statistics, density).

## Findings by paper

(appended as confirmed)

### A. The direct precedent for Q1: vegetation height from the SPREAD of the whole return-height distribution (short vegetation, discrete return)

**Davenport, I.J., Bradbury, R.B., Anderson, G.Q.A., Hayman, G.R.F., Krebs, J.R., Mason, D.C., Wilson, J.D., Veck, N.J. (2000). Improving bird population models using airborne remote sensing. Int. J. Remote Sensing 21(13-14), 2705-2717.** [ABS; DOI not retrieved this session, Taylor & Francis] Farmland near Oxford, UK; airborne laser altimeter with <10 cm height accuracy. Found "a simple link between mean crop height and the spread of detrended return heights within a field," giving crop height to better than 10 cm. This is the origin of the "spread of all returns = height" idea for sub-meter vegetation.

**Cobby, D.M., Mason, D.C., Davenport, I.J. (2001). Image processing of airborne scanning laser altimetry data for improved river flood modelling. ISPRS J. Photogramm. Remote Sens. 56, 121-138.** [CITED-IN Hopkinson 2004; DOI not retrieved; a 2001 SPIE 4171 version by Cobby, Mason, Davenport & Horritt is confirmed, DOI 10.1117/12.413925] Per Hopkinson 2004: crop vegetation up to ~1.2 m predicted from the standard deviation of topographically detrended returns with a LOGARITHMIC fit, r2 0.8, RMSE 0.14 m, valid only for Ht < ~2 m; the SPIE abstract gives vegetation height "to an accuracy of +-14 cm for grass and cereal crops" with a 3-class (short/intermediate/tall) segmentation before height extraction. Summer, leaf-on only (Straatsma & Middelkoop 2007 note this limit). <!-- spelling: ok (verbatim title) -->

**Hopkinson, C., Lim, K., Chasmer, L.E., Treitz, P., Creed, I.F., Gynan, C. (2004). Wetland grass to plantation forest - estimating vegetation height from the standard deviation of lidar frequency distributions. Int. Arch. Photogramm. Remote Sens. Spatial Inf. Sci. XXXVI-8/W2, 288-294.** [FULL; conference, not peer reviewed; no DOI] Nine Optech ALTM (1225/1210/2050) datasets, 2000-2002, four Canadian sites, 12 vegetation types from <1 m to 24 m; point spacing 0.8-2.0 m (up to 5 pulses/m2 at Utikuma with 200 % overlap). Metric: LSD = standard deviation of the detrended heights of ALL first and last returns (ground included, no classification) over a plot or transect. Result: Ht = M x LSD with M = 2.5 over all 68 plots (r2 0.95, RMSE 1.8 m); for the three SHORT classes (aquatic marsh, grass and herbs, low shrubs, all < 2 m; n = 14 transects, Utikuma Lake, August 2002, ALTM 2050) M = 2.7, r2 0.77, RMSE 0.15 m. Floor: over flat bare ground LSD ~0.07 m, so the model's minimum height is ~0.19 m, i.e. it cannot see vegetation below ~0.2 m. Shape statements verbatim: "Short vegetation, however, tends not to display a bimodal distribution ... attributed to: a) more homogeneous vegetation structure from ground to canopy surface (Cobby et al. 2001); b) limitations in older generation lidar sensors preventing first and last pulse separation for ranges below ~4 m"; "For the short vegetation transects sampled (Figure 1B), the laser pulse distributions from ground to canopy tend to display a single strong mode with an otherwise irregular distribution. All vegetation classes display at least one major mode with a frequency ranging between 15% and 50% of all laser pulse data." Why SD rather than max or a percentile: it uses the entire column and is insensitive to density and to the number of returns; the authors also note slope inflates LSD "regardless of vegetation height." Relevance to Elba: this is the one airborne discrete-return study that treats the WHOLE height distribution of a sub-2 m sward as the measurement, and it reduces the shape to a single number (SD); it reports the single-mode form Andy sees in 2008 and that the mode sits at a "major" 15-50 % of returns, but it does not use the mode position, the ground shelf, or any higher moment.

**Luo, S., Wang, C., Pan, F., Xi, X., Li, G., Nie, S., Xia, S. (2015). Estimation of wetland vegetation height and leaf area index using airborne laser scanning data. Ecological Indicators 48, 550-559. DOI 10.1016/j.ecolind.2014.09.024** [ABS, full abstract from AGRIS; Crossref-verified DOI] Short wetland vegetation (site and species not in the abstract; the group's other work is Yellow River Delta Phragmites/Suaeda/Tamarix). Raw point accuracy 0.09 m, DEM RMSE 0.15 m. "A raster canopy height model did not estimate the height of short vegetation accurately," but regressing field height on the standard deviation of detrended lidar heights in a 1.50 m radius gave R2 0.85, RMSE 0.14 m (validation R2 0.84, RMSE 0.14 m). LAI from a laser penetration index (ground returns / all returns, definition not in abstract) gave R2 0.70; LAI from the SD-predicted height R2 0.79. Relevance: an independent (2015, Chinese delta wetland) replication of the Hopkinson/Cobby SD law for short wetland vegetation, and the one paper found that uses a PENETRATION INDEX as a density (LAI) proxy in short wetland vegetation; the penetration index was the weaker of the two.

### B. Shrub-steppe: where the single return sits and how far below the top

**Streutker, D.R., Glenn, N.F. (2006). LiDAR measurement of sagebrush steppe vegetation heights. Remote Sensing of Environment 102(1-2), 135-145. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2006.02.011** [ABS; Crossref-verified] SE Idaho sagebrush steppe; first-return-derived heights "moderately well correlated" with field heights but "uniformly underestimate" them, attributed to "signal threshold limits within the LiDAR sensor" (the return triggers inside the canopy, not at its top). The 2005 ASPRS preprint by the same authors (ISU GIS Center PDF, downloaded; 15 pp.) reports r = 0.72 for sagebrush height and suggests "a scaling factor of two" may be needed.

**Mitchell, J.J., Glenn, N.F., Sankey, T.T., Derryberry, D.R., Anderson, M.O., Hruska, R.C. (2011). Small-footprint lidar estimations of sagebrush canopy characteristics. Photogrammetric Engineering & Remote Sensing 77(5), 521-530.** [ABS] 9.46 points/m2 discrete return; sagebrush height predicted with R2 0.84-0.86 "but with a consistent underestimation of approximately 30 percent"; first lidar shape descriptors (crown diameter/area R2 0.65-0.78). **Glenn, N.F., Spaete, L.P., Sankey, T.T., Derryberry, D.R., Hardegree, S.P., Mitchell, J.J. (2011). Errors in LiDAR-derived shrub height and crown area on sloped terrain. J. Arid Environments 75(4), 377-382.** [ABS; DOI not retrieved] Four height methods; hillslope has small effect on height; crown area underestimated by a mean of 49 %, improving above >4 points/m2. Relevance: the "reads 30 % short of the top" number for a 0.3-1 m woody canopy is the shrub analog of the "one third of the way up" placement of the grass single return (Rogers 2016, Hopkinson 2005 in LIT_LOW_VEGETATION_LIDAR.md), from the opposite direction (top-down for shrubs, bottom-up for grass).

**Related caution [ABS]:** a sagebrush community-type classification (Rangeland Ecology & Management 64(1), 2011, Boise State group) found lidar heights differed among all community types "except for herbaceous-dominated communities compared to low sagebrush-dominated communities": at ~1-9 points/m2, grass and low shrub are not separable on height statistics.

### C. The closest analog to the November 2008 flight: senescent herbaceous floodplain, leaf-off, with the ground/vegetation split taken FROM THE HISTOGRAM SHAPE

**Straatsma, M.W., Middelkoop, H. (2007). Extracting structural characteristics of herbaceous floodplain vegetation under leaf-off conditions using airborne laser scanner data. Int. J. Remote Sensing 28(11), 2447-2467. DOI 10.1080/01431160600928633** [FULL; ITC PDF; Crossref-verified] Lower Rhine floodplains (Duursche/Afferdensche/Gamerense Waarden), FLI-MAP II helicopter lidar, first pulse, March 2001 and March 2003 (winter, vegetation "in senescence"), 12-75 points/m2, 42 plots of >=200 m2 of sedge, thistle, clover and grass with field heights 0.26-1.66 m. Three ways to label returns as vegetation vs ground, two of them read off the SHAPE of the per-plot height histogram (2 cm bins): (1) a fixed 0.15 m threshold; (2) the "inflection method": "the point of maximum concave-up curvature in the upper limb of the histogram" after fitting a Harris function, which fell "in all cases lower than 15 cm, and typically around 5 cm"; (3) a Gaussian fitted to the ground peak with the excess above the fit labeled vegetation. Height: with inflection labeling, D90-D98 plus the SD and variance of vegetation points all correlate r > 0.85 with field height; the adopted model Hv = 1.06 D95 + 0.40 (GWlow, R2 0.88, RSE 0.11 m), 2.51 D95 + 0.11 (DWADW, R2 0.76) and 1.47 D95 + 0.28 (GWhigh, R2 0.74): the coefficients differ significantly between surveys (flying height, gain, laser age), so the law is not portable without reference plots. Density: the percentage index PI (vegetation points / all points, i.e. one minus the ground-return fraction) predicts hydrodynamic vegetation density with R2 0.51 (threshold labeling), 0.49 (Gaussian) but only 0.09 with inflection labeling. Correlations hold down to ~15 points/m2 and degrade below. The authors note the slope Hopkinson 2004 found is three times Davenport 2000's and attribute it to dense crops returning from the canopy top versus open winter herbs that "generate a weak" canopy signal. Relevance: (a) this is the one study of a senescent floodplain sward under leaf-off conditions; (b) it fits a Gaussian to the ground mode and reads vegetation as the residual, which is a shape decomposition of the kind Andy is asking about, used as a labeling step rather than as a descriptor; (c) its inflection at ~5 cm above the DTM is the same object as the Elba "thin shelf" of ground returns; (d) it needed 12-75 points/m2 and did not test anything like 1 point/m2.

### D. The Zlinszky / Vienna wetland and grassland series (Balaton reed, Sopron meadows, Hortobagy steppe) and the Dutch follow-up

**Zlinszky, A., Mucke, W., Lehner, H., Briese, C., Pfeifer, N. (2012). Categorizing wetland vegetation by airborne laser scanning on Lake Balaton and Kis-Balaton, Hungary. Remote Sensing 4(6), 1617-1650. DOI 10.3390/rs4061617** [FULL; TU Wien PDF] Leica ALS50, 1064 nm, August 2010, 1 pt/m2, 22 cm footprint, up to four echoes, AGC-modulated amplitude. Five point-cloud rasters, all simple: NDSM height (highest point per 2.5 m cell minus lowest point per 10 m cell; "absolute vegetation heights were not calculated"); Sigma Z (SD of residuals from a plane fitted to the 8 nearest points within 2.5 m, the small-scale roughness, "narrower than in trees and shrubs but wider than over mown lawns"); grid variance (3x3 variance of a 1 m moving-plane surface, meant to capture "density inhomogeneities related to vegetation stress [that] cause variations in the penetration of the signal and thus the vertical distribution of echoes"); DTM variance (slope proxy); reflectance from AGC-corrected amplitude; and the dropout count (missing echoes over water). Expert decision tree with hand-set thresholds from signature histograms (Table 1): Carex = reflectance > 0.22 AND NDSM < 0.95 m AND grid variance < 0.04; Typha = reflectance < 0.155 AND grid variance < 0.3; die-back reed = reflectance 0.07-0.34 AND dropouts > 3; ruderal reed = reflectance > 0.4; stressed reed = grid variance > 0.2; healthy reed = reflectance < 0.4 AND grid variance < 0.2. Accuracy over 775 photo-interpreted points: overall 82.5 %, kappa 0.80; Typha user's/producer's 72.9/88.6 %, Carex 82.9/60.4 % (n = 48), die-back reed 62.5 %, reed-as-a-whole 91.6/94.0 %, Typha+Carex vs reed 81.0/84.6 %. The genus separation is physical: Typha's vertical leaves give high penetration and LOW reflectance; Carex's lax leaves reflect most of the pulse (HIGH reflectance, low height); reed's stalks (up to 4 m) give intermediate penetration. The confusions are instructive: tall Carex -> reed, low-density reed -> Typha, i.e. the separators are height, reflectance and roughness, not a distribution shape. No moment higher than variance is used; the paper states that at 1 pt/m2 "low canopy penetration" limits what can be read and recommends "full waveform ALS recording or sampling with a higher point density." Relevance: the canonical ALS wetland-genus paper works at the 2008 density and separates emergent genera 1-4 m tall; it says nothing about swards < 0.5 m, where NDSM and Sigma Z both collapse toward the sensor noise.

**Zlinszky, A., Schroiff, A., Kania, A., Deak, B., Mucke, W., Vari, A., Szekely, B., Pfeifer, N. (2014). Categorizing grassland vegetation with full-waveform airborne laser scanning: a feasibility study for detecting Natura 2000 habitat types. Remote Sensing 6(9), 8056-8087. DOI 10.3390/rs6098056** [FULL; already cited in LIT_LOW_VEGETATION_LIDAR.md for echo width and mowing; the variable set and importances are new here] Riegl LMS-Q680 full waveform, 1550 nm, 12.8 echoes/m2, 0.25 m footprint, leaf-on July 2011 and leaf-off March 2012, Sopron, Hungary; grass layers 0-25 cm (rosettes), 50-60 cm (middle) and 120-150 cm (tall grasses). Twelve 0.5 m rasters: mean calibrated reflectance, mean echo width, NDSM height (second-highest point per cell), Sigma Z (SD from a plane through the 8 nearest points within 1.5 m), min. and max. openness, each for leaf-on and leaf-off, plus the leaf-on minus leaf-off reflectance difference. Random forest on 140 plots: 5 classes 75.5 % overall (kappa 0.66), 10 classes 68.0 % (kappa 0.64). Most important bands: leaf-off echo width, the leaf-on minus leaf-off reflectance difference, and leaf-off NDSM. Verbatim on the height metric: "the height of grassland vegetation is strongly underestimated. In lowland hay meadows or dry grasslands where the vegetation height at the time of flying was around 50-120 cm, we found that the leaf-on NDSM shows heights around 20-30 cm"; on roughness: "Sigma Z does not enhance very fine differences in point heights: very flat surfaces such as asphalt roads have quite similar values to mown meadows, both in the range of 2-5 cm"; on echo width: "Differences in the order of magnitude of 0.1 ns in mean echo width are typical between mown and abandoned meadows, or Molinia-dominated patches and lowland hay meadows ... at the very edge of use" and echo width "varies slightly with the scan angle, increasing by 0.1-0.2 ns towards the strip edges." Relevance: the best grassland ALS classification in the literature separates MANAGEMENT and habitat type, not species, at 12.8 echoes/m2 with full waveform, and its strongest discriminators are a within-pulse quantity (echo width) and a two-season reflectance difference, neither available from the 2008 discrete data; the discrete per-cell roughness (Sigma Z) bottoms out at 2-5 cm for mown grass, which is the Elba 2021 NMAD.

**Zlinszky, A., Deak, B., Kania, A., Schroiff, A., Pfeifer, N. (2015). Mapping Natura 2000 habitat conservation status in a Pannonic salt steppe with airborne laser scanning. Remote Sensing 7(3), 2991-3019. DOI 10.3390/rs70302991** [FULL; mdpi-res PDF, skimmed by grep] Same sensor family, 10 pt/m2, summer and winter, Hortobagy short alkali grassland. Rasters: NDSM, mean and max normalized height, sigma_z, sigma_0 and variance of the normalized cloud, mean reflectance and its variance at two kernel sizes, mean and max echo width and their variances. Litter accumulation was read from the leaf-on minus leaf-off reflectance difference (threshold > 0.3 unfavorable), and the paper concedes "the level of litter accumulation is difficult to determine by ALS in short grass steppes." Relevance: litter/thatch, the state of the Elba sward in November 2008, is detected here only radiometrically and only with two seasons.

**Alexander, C., Deak, B., Kania, A., Mucke, W., Heilmeier, H. (2015). Classification of vegetation in an open landscape using full-waveform airborne laser scanner data. Int. J. Applied Earth Observation and Geoinformation 41, 76-87. DOI 10.1016/j.jag.2015.04.014** [ABS; Crossref-verified] Hortobagy alkali landscape; 18 leaf-on full-waveform variables in a random forest; Level 1 (dry grassland, wetland, weeds, woody, man-made) kappa 0.79, finest Level 4 kappa 0.51. The abstract states "ALS data have only rarely been used for the classification of grassland vegetation."

**Koma, Z., Zlinszky, A., Beko, L., Burai, P., Seijmonsbergen, A.C., Kissling, W.D. (2021). Quantifying 3D vegetation structure in wetlands using differently measured airborne laser scanning data. Ecological Indicators 127, 107752. DOI 10.1016/j.ecolind.2021.107752** [ABS, UvA DARE page; Crossref-verified] Lakes Balaton, Ferto and Tisza; full-waveform vs discrete, 4 vs 21 pt/m2; eight metrics (radiometric, cover, height, vertical variability; names not on the page). Height R2 0.84 (FWF alone), 0.67 pooled; biomass R2 0.20-0.22; LAI R2 0.08-0.30. Verdict: "FWF ALS captures more vegetation structural details in dense reed and marshland vegetation." A companion (Koma et al. 2021, Ecography, "Niche separation of wetland birds revealed from airborne laser scanning", DOI not retrieved) defines reedbed on country-wide Dutch ALS by a VV_p95 window of 1-3 m, i.e. a single percentile. Relevance: even at 21 pt/m2 the vertical-variability metrics do not carry biomass or LAI in reed; height is what ALS measures well.

### E. Phragmites and other reed/marsh work (discrete return)

**Onojeghuo, A.O., Blackburn, G.A. (2011). Optimising the use of hyperspectral and LiDAR data for mapping reedbed habitats. Remote Sensing of Environment 115(8), 2025-2034. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2011.04.004** [ABS; Crossref-verified] Cumbria, UK. The lidar contribution is a single mask: a 3 m threshold on the CHM raised reedbed accuracy by 11 %. **Onojeghuo & Blackburn (2013). Characterising reedbeds using LiDAR data: potential and limitations. IEEE JSTARS 6(2), 935-941. DOI 10.1109/JSTARS.2012.2212235** [ABS; Lancaster eprint; Crossref-verified] Leica ALS50 leaf-off; first-return CHM vs field height RMSE 0.47 m, mean difference 0.09 m (~5 % of mean height); "few multiple LiDAR returns and few ground returns" in reed; intensity suffers specular reflection. No distribution shape metric. <!-- spelling: ok (verbatim titles) -->

**Luo, S., Wang, C., Xi, X., Pan, F., Qian, M., Peng, D., Nie, S., Qin, H., Lin, Y. (2017). Retrieving aboveground biomass of wetland Phragmites australis (common reed) using a combination of airborne discrete-return LiDAR and hyperspectral data. Int. J. Applied Earth Observation and Geoinformation 58, 107-117. DOI 10.1016/j.jag.2017.01.016** [ABS plus secondary citation; Crossref-verified] Point-based (not CHM) height, density, canopy and intensity metrics; best single lidar metric H_p99; lidar-only models used mean first-return height and the 10th/90th percentiles; combined lidar + MSAVI R2 0.648, RMSE 167.5 g/m2 (figure from a 2025 citing paper, not confirmed against the original).

**Corti Meneses, N., Baier, S., Geist, J., Schneider, T. (2017). Evaluation of green-LiDAR data for mapping extent, density and height of aquatic reed beds at Lake Chiemsee, Bavaria, Germany. Remote Sensing 9(12), 1308. DOI 10.3390/rs9121308** [FULL; mdpi-res PDF] ~200 points/m2 green (532 nm) bathymetric lidar; OPALS point features: rank (relative vertical position of a point within its 0.2-0.5 m cylinder neighborhood, 0 = local min, 100 = local max) and local point density. Verbatim: "Cross-sections revealed that height and vertical distribution of points are important factors to differentiate dense and sparse reed beds"; "points were equally distributed in sparse stocks and accumulated in the upper canopy for dense stocks"; sparse reed heights 1.80-2.40 m, 2 m used as the split. Height RMSE 0.1 m. Relevance: the one reed paper that states a distribution-SHAPE rule (uniform vs top-heavy) and ties it to stem density, but at 200 points/m2 on 2 m reed and as a rule in a decision scheme, not a fitted descriptor.

**Wang, C., Morris, J.T., Smith, E. (2024). A Lidar Biomass Index of tidal marshes from drone lidar point cloud. Remote Sensing 16(11), 1823. DOI 10.3390/rs16111823** [FULL; mdpi-res PDF, skimmed] Spartina alterniflora monoculture, drone lidar, hundreds of points/m2, "predominantly in single returns in marsh fields." Lidar_BI = allometric stem biomass (from the lidar maximum height H_max, which "was dramatically lower than in-field measurements, but the two variables held a strong linear relationship") multiplied by the vegetation point count per 0.5 m cell; R2 0.5 against clipped biomass vs 0.4 for NDVI. Relevance: the vegetation point COUNT per cell (the complement of the ground fraction) is used as a plant-density proxy, with the explicit caveat that it "is not a measure of the stem density of plants" because it varies with sensor and flight.

**Pinton, D., Canestrelli, A., Wilkinson, B., Ifju, P., Ortega, A. (2020). A new algorithm for estimating ground elevation and vegetation characteristics in coastal salt marshes from high-resolution UAV-based LiDAR point clouds. Earth Surface Processes and Landforms 45(14). DOI 10.1002/esp.4992** [ABS; Crossref-verified] ~500 points/m2 over Spartina, Little Sapelo Island; "directly converts the 3D distribution of UAV LiDAR-derived points into vegetation density and height, as well as ground elevation"; 40 x 40 cm maps; slope raises errors. The 2021 Remote Sensing follow-up (13(22), 4506) found RGB photogrammetry adds nothing to the lidar estimates. Relevance: the most complete "distribution -> ground + height + density" inversion found, but calibrated on one species at 500 points/m2.

**Hladik, C., Alber, M. (2012). Accuracy assessment and correction of a LIDAR-derived salt marsh digital elevation model. Remote Sensing of Environment 121, 224-235. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2012.01.018** [ABS; Crossref-verified] Sapelo Island; species/cover-class-specific DEM correction factors for ten classes (Spartina height forms, Juncus, Salicornia, Batis, etc.); the 2013 follow-up (Hladik, Schalles & Alber, RSE 139) applies hyperspectral cover classes to pick the correction. Relevance: the species fingerprint runs the OTHER way in this literature: the lidar offset is predicted FROM the species map, never the species from the lidar distribution.

### F. Shape descriptors in the forest/waveform literature (what a "full shape factor" has meant there)

**Harding, D.J., Lefsky, M.A., Parker, G.G., Blair, J.B. (2001). Laser altimeter canopy height profiles: methods and validation for closed-canopy, broadleaf forests. Remote Sensing of Environment 76(3), 283-297. DOI 10.1016/S0034-4257(00)00210-8** [ABS; Crossref-verified author list] SLICER waveforms over Maryland deciduous stands; the canopy height profile (CHP) is the MacArthur-Horn (1969) transform of the cumulative waveform, i.e. the full vertical foliage-density function, validated against field CHPs. **Lovell, J.L., Jupp, D.L.B., Culvenor, D.S., Coops, N.C. (2003). Using airborne and ground-based ranging lidar to measure canopy structure in Australian forests. Canadian J. Remote Sensing 29(5), 607-622. DOI 10.5589/m03-026** [CITED-IN; Crossref-verified] The "apparent foliage profile" from gap fraction, airborne and ground. **Coops, N.C., Hilker, T., Wulder, M.A., St-Onge, B., Newnham, G., Siggins, A., Trofymow, J.A. (2007). Estimating canopy structure of Douglas-fir forest stands from discrete-return LiDAR. Trees 21(3), 295-310. DOI 10.1007/s00468-006-0119-6** [ABS; Crossref-verified] 0.7 hits/m2 discrete return; foliage profiles fitted with a two-parameter WEIBULL to both field and lidar profiles; mean height r2 0.85; the Weibull shape/scale pair is the "full shape factor" of this tradition. A Eucalyptus understory paper (Scientia Agricola, revistas.usp.br, [ABS]) splits the profile at 0.5 of height, fits a Weibull to each layer, and detects understory presence from the parameters with accuracy 0.96. **Drake, J.B., Dubayah, R.O., Clark, D.B., et al. (2002). Estimation of tropical forest structural characteristics using large-footprint lidar. RSE 79, 305-319. DOI 10.1016/S0034-4257(01)00281-4** [ABS] HOME = height of median energy, a single quantile of the waveform; biomass r2 0.89. **Blair, J.B., Rabine, D.L., Hofton, M.A. (1999). The Laser Vegetation Imaging Sensor. ISPRS J. 54, 115-122. DOI 10.1016/S0924-2716(99)00002-7** and **Dubayah, R. et al. (2020). The Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation. Science of Remote Sensing 1, 100002. DOI 10.1016/j.srs.2020.100002** [ABS] define the RH metrics: heights at integer percentiles (1 % steps in GEDI L2A) of cumulative waveform energy above the center of the LOWEST mode; over single-mode short vegetation "RH values can fall below zero" (ORNL/NASA documentation), and global GEDI/ICESat-2 heights are "highly biased (>3 m) for reference heights under 5 m" (search-level, IOP 2024). DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2010.11.008** [ABS; Crossref-verified] Bins discrete returns (count or intensity) in height, spline-smooths, and takes waveform-style metrics; forest only; no low-vegetation application of pseudo-waveforms was found. **Clawges, R., Vierling, K., Vierling, L., Rowell, E. (2008). RSE 112(5), 2064-2073. Relevance: every "complete" shape descriptor in the literature (CHP, Weibull pair, RH vector, FHD, echo width) was built for canopies meters tall, where the profile spans many vertical bins; none has been applied to a sward whose entire profile is 0.1-0.4 m, i.e. one to three pulse widths.

## Synthesis

## Synthesis

**Q1. Is there a "full shape factor" for a return-height distribution?** In the forest and waveform literature, yes, under several names, and all of them presume a canopy several meters tall: the MacArthur-Horn canopy height profile (Harding 2001; Lovell 2003), a two-parameter Weibull fitted to that profile (Coops 2007 and the understory split), the RH percentile vector of cumulative energy (LVIS, GEDI), the Shannon entropy of the height histogram (foliage height diversity, Clawges 2008), per-echo width and amplitude from Gaussian decomposition (Wagner 2006; Mallet & Bretar 2009), and pseudo-waveforms built from discrete returns (Muss 2011). For vegetation under about 2 m the literature reduces the whole distribution to ONE number: its standard deviation (Davenport 2000; Cobby 2001; Hopkinson 2004, M = 2.7, R2 0.77, RMSE 0.15 m, floor ~0.19 m; Luo 2015, R2 0.85), a high percentile (Straatsma & Middelkoop 2007, D95; Luo 2017, p99; Koma 2021, p95), or a ground fraction (Straatsma's percentage index, R2 0.51 for density; Luo's penetration index, R2 0.70 for LAI; Wang 2024's point count). Hopkinson 2004 describes the sub-2 m form exactly as Andy sees it in 2008, "a single strong mode with an otherwise irregular distribution," and Straatsma reads the ground/vegetation split from the histogram's curvature (inflection ~5 cm) or from the residual above a Gaussian ground peak. Nobody in the short-vegetation literature has gone past the second moment: no skewness, kurtosis, entropy, quantile ratio, Weibull pair, mixture decomposition or functional PCA of a sward's return-height distribution was found, at any point density.

**Q2. Has anyone fingerprinted herbaceous, wetland or invasive-grass vegetation from the distribution?** Partly, and always with height, reflectance and roughness rather than shape. Zlinszky 2012 separates Phragmites, Typha and Carex at 1 pt/m2 (82.5 %, kappa 0.80) with NDSM height, Sigma Z, a grid variance and AGC reflectance, by expert thresholds, on emergents 1-4 m tall; the confusions (tall Carex -> reed, thin reed -> Typha) show the separators are stature and density. Zlinszky 2014 separates MANAGEMENT classes of hay meadows (mown, abandoned, Molinia) at 12.8 echoes/m2 only with full-waveform echo width (0.1 ns differences) and a two-season reflectance difference, with NDSM reading 20-30 cm on 50-120 cm grass and Sigma Z flat at 2-5 cm. Corti Meneses 2017 states a distribution-shape rule (uniform through the canopy for sparse reed, top-heavy for dense) at 200 points/m2 on 2 m reed. Srolleru & Potuckova 2024 reach species level for one tussock grass at 800 points/m2 with monthly flights. Reed canary grass has no lidar paper at all (Landsat and habitat models only); Phragmites has height and biomass papers (Onojeghuo 2011/2013; Luo 2017) but no shape fingerprint; sedge/fen work (Korpela 2009) finds sward intensity reads surface moisture, not vegetation. Salt-marsh work runs the inference backwards, from a species map to a lidar offset (Hladik & Alber 2012).

**What is not in the literature, as far as this search reached:** (1) any return-height distribution analysis of a sward below ~0.5 m at ~1 pt/m2, where NDSM, Sigma Z and the SD law all sit at the noise floor; (2) any attempt to fingerprint herbaceous species or state (green vs senescent, standing vs lodged, thatch) from the SHAPE of a discrete-return height distribution rather than from its height or reflectance; (3) any two-epoch comparison in which the distribution's mode position above RTK ground (0.13 m vs 0.2-0.4 m) is itself the measurement; (4) any 1064 nm intensity contrast of senesced versus green grass. The Elba per-point, RTK-referenced distributions, with a resolved ground shelf and a mode whose height varies with place in 2008, are therefore beyond what has been published; the nearest precedents are Hopkinson 2004 and Straatsma 2007, both of which would predict that the 2021 narrow peak (SD ~3-4 cm) is read as "no vegetation" and that the 2008 broad population (SD well above 7 cm) is read as a ~0.3-0.5 m canopy.
