## Question

At Elba (Whitewater River floodplain, MN) the per-1 m-cell distribution of 3DEP May 2021 discrete
return heights (1,064 nm, ~17 pulses/m^2) has a cover-specific shape: an exponential-flanked core
over the hay sward (e-folding 1.5-2 cm), a shoulder 12-13 cm down where 1-4 % of pulses reach the
soil, a 0.2-1 m layer at tree lines, a steeper sub-soil tail in open sward (2.9 cm e-folding) than
near trees (5.8 cm), and intensity falling with depth. Earlier ridgeline work found ground-return
tails exponential under forest and power-law on bare/open ground. Two questions:

1. Who has identified CHARACTERISTIC RETURN STRUCTURES tied to land cover or land-surface
   roughness from airborne or UAV discrete-return point clouds?
2. Who has described the per-cell or per-object return-height SHAPE (moments, skewness, kurtosis,
   entropy, percentiles, echo ratio, penetration ratio, planarity/roughness, intensity and
   number-of-returns statistics) and used it to classify or characterize cover, especially
   NON-FOREST cover (grassland, hay, crops, marsh, shrub, bare soil, gravel, water, riparian
   mosaics)? Has anyone printed return-height HISTOGRAMS per class, fitted a family per class, or
   read a histogram TAIL (exponential, power-law) as a cover signature?

Fusion with optical or radar data is a second agent's brief and is left out here.

### A. Point-cloud land-cover classification with per-cell or per-object distribution features (the feature-relevance literature)

**Charaniya, A.P., Manduchi, R., Lodha, S.K. (2004). Supervised parametric classification of aerial LiDAR data. IEEE CVPR Workshops (CVPRW 2004), Washington DC. DOI 10.1109/CVPR.2004.446 [DOI UNVERIFIED; Crossref did not return it]** [ABS + secondary (mlanthology record, CSDN translation of the paper)] UC Santa Cruz campus, airborne discrete-return lidar gridded to cells. Five per-cell features: normalized height (DSM minus DEM), HEIGHT VARIATION within the cell (the per-cell spread of return heights), intensity, the first-minus-last return height difference ("multiple returns"), and image luminance. Each of four classes (trees, grass, roads, buildings) is modeled as a MIXTURE OF GAUSSIANS fitted by EM (four components per class); classification is maximum likelihood, then a spatial-coherence pass. Accuracies 66-84 % depending on the feature set; 85 % train-all/test-all. Relevance: the earliest per-cell "shape" feature in the lidar land-cover literature is the height variation (a second moment) and the earliest per-class distribution model is a Gaussian mixture of the FEATURE vector, not of the return heights themselves. Grass is one of the four classes and is separated from roads mainly by intensity and from trees by height variation; no histogram of grass return heights is published.

**Brennan, R., Webster, T.L. (2006). Object-oriented land cover classification of lidar-derived surfaces. Canadian Journal of Remote Sensing 32(2), 162-172. DOI 10.5589/m06-015** [CITED-IN: a 2007 Portland State course summary (Craver) of the paper; the CJRS text was not opened] Annapolis Valley, Nova Scotia; a 2 x 1 km subset of 1,263,780 points (density not stated in the summary). Four lidar surfaces: DSM, DEM, normalized height, intensity (all returns); a multiple-echo surface is listed in the abstract. eCognition segmentation and a rule base using elevation, intensity, shape, texture and slope; intensity is read as moisture ("lower intensity = higher moisture content of soil / vegetation" per the summary). TEN classes, overall 94.31 %. No higher distribution moment; the per-object features are means and textures of rasters. Relevance: the standard early object-based lidar-only land-cover result; shape of the height distribution enters only as raster texture.

**Antonarakis, A.S., Richards, K.S., Brasington, J. (2008). Object-based land cover classification using airborne LiDAR. Remote Sensing of Environment 112(6), 2988-2998. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2008.02.004** [ABS; Aberystwyth portal abstract; full text embargoed at Sussex; 351 citations per Semantic Scholar] Three meanders of the Garonne and Allier (France). Supervised object-based classification from lidar ELEVATION and INTENSITY rasters plus "point distribution frequency criteria" (the abstract's phrase), with and without ground returns in vegetated areas. Classes: five riparian forest types (planted and natural stands of different ages, 66-98 %), short vegetation and bare earth (each averaging above 90 %); overall 95 % (ground influence kept) and 94 % (ground points removed). The keywords include "vegetation roughness" and "hydraulic modelling", i.e. the classification is a roughness map for flood models. The abstract (figshare record, full text) names the shape features only as "point distribution frequency criteria"; Crosilla et al. 2011 (ISPRS Archives XXXVIII-5/W12, read in full, section G below) describe the method as [CITED-IN]: "Antonarikis et al. (2008) subdivided the whole area into cells of small dimension and for each cell the computation of skewness and kurtosis of the points first and last pulses have been computed. Final classification results from the combination of several parameters." So the per-cell SKEWNESS and KURTOSIS of the first- and last-pulse height distributions are confirmed at second hand; the per-class values, the cell size and the point density were not retrieved (full text embargoed). This is the one riparian-floodplain paper in which third- and fourth-moment shape features of the per-cell return distribution separate short vegetation and bare earth from forest types. **Im, J., Jensen, J.R., Hodgson, M.E. (2008). Object-based land cover classification using high-posting-density LiDAR data. GIScience & Remote Sensing 45(2), 209-228. DOI 10.2747/1548-1603.45.2.209** [bibliographic only; abstract page 403] Crossref-verified. Content not retrieved this session; cited here so the brief's list is closed, with the status stated.

**Chehata, N., Guo, L., Mallet, C. (2009). Airborne lidar feature selection for urban classification using random forests. ISPRS Archives XXXVIII-3/W8 (Laserscanning 09, Paris), 207-212.** [FULL; isprs.org PDF] Biberach, Germany, Riegl LMS-Q560, 500 m AGL, 0.25 m footprint, 2.5 pts/m2; 797,364 labeled points split evenly train/test; features computed per point in a cylindrical neighborhood and rasterized at 0.5 m. TWENTY-ONE features in five groups: height-based (height above the lowest point in 15 m, first-last height difference, HEIGHT VARIANCE), echo-based (number of echoes, normalized echo number), eigenvalue-based (lambda1-3, anisotropy, planarity, sphericity, linearity), local-plane (deviation angle of the normal, its variance, plane residuals, distance to plane) and full-waveform (amplitude, echo width, cross-section, generalized-Gaussian shape alpha). Classes: buildings, vegetation, NATURAL GROUND ("grass, sand, and bare-earth regions"), artificial ground. Random forest with backward elimination; 17 retained features give 94.35 % overall; a 6-feature set still gives a low OOB error. Verbatim on relevance: "the most important features are height-based: the height difference and the height variance. Echo-based features are not important for urban scene classification when using other attributes that describe more accurately the local distribution of 3D points such as eigenvalue-based or 3D plane-based features." For vegetation the per-group winners are sphericity, plane residuals and echo width; for natural ground "many variables seem to be important ... interpreted with caution due to the few number of corresponding training samples." Confusion of vegetation with artificial ground occurs where "in non-dense vegetated areas, the lidar beam is likely to reach the ground underneath and the resulting waveform has mixed properties." Relevance: the canonical ranking of per-cell distribution features at 2.5 pts/m2; the second moment (height variance) and the eigenvalue shape of the local cloud do the work, the echo counts do not, and natural ground is the class the method handles worst. No skewness, kurtosis or percentile features are in the 21.

ISPRS Journal of Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing 66(1), 56-66. DOI 10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2010.08.007** [ABS; HAL record] Journal extension of Chehata 2009 with the multispectral image added (fusion side left to the other agent). The abstract: random forests "provide measures of feature importance for each class"; a margin-based confidence measure confirms feature relevance; "the relevance of full-waveform lidar features is demonstrated for building and vegetation area discrimination." Same Biberach data, same four classes.

**Mallet, C., Bretar, F., Roux, M., Soergel, U., Heipke, C. (2011). Relevance assessment of full-waveform lidar data for urban area classification. ISPRS Journal of Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing 66(6 Suppl.), S71-S84. DOI 10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2011.09.008** [ABS; IP Paris portal] Three classes (building, ground, vegetation); full-waveform features compared with "a large variety of multiple-pulse features" by three feature-selection methods; accuracies around 95 %; "radiometric features (the cross-section and backscatter coefficient) significantly contribute"; and the negative result: "Features extracted from the non Gaussian modelling of the echoes are not relevant for the discrimination of vegetation, ground, and buildings in urban areas." Relevance: within-pulse echo SHAPE (generalized-Gaussian, lognormal, Nakagami, Burr families, already in `GROUND_RETURN_PDF_LITERATURE.md`) does not separate cover classes; the between-pulse height distribution and the radiometry do. <!-- spelling: ok (verbatim title) -->

**Weinmann, M., Jutzi, B., Hinz, S., Mallet, C. (2015). Semantic point cloud interpretation based on optimal neighborhoods, relevant features and efficient classifiers. ISPRS Journal of Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing 105, 286-304. DOI 10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2015.01.016** [ABS; KIT/TUM records] Mobile and terrestrial benchmarks (Oakland, Paris-rue-Madame), 7 neighborhood definitions, 21 geometric features (the eigenvalue set: linearity, planarity, sphericity, omnivariance, anisotropy, eigenentropy, change of curvature, plus height and density features), 7 feature-selection approaches, 10 classifiers. Relevance: the modern canonical list of per-neighborhood SHAPE features is covariance-eigenvalue based; none is a moment of the height histogram beyond variance, and the test scenes are urban.

**Yan, W.Y., Shaker, A., El-Ashmawy, N. (2015). Urban land cover classification using airborne LiDAR data: A review. Remote Sensing of Environment 158, 295-310. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2014.11.001** [ABS; PolyU record] Reviews a decade of lidar-only and lidar-led urban land-cover work by "data configuration, feature spaces, classification techniques, and radiometric calibration/correction", with the overall accuracies. The feature spaces it tabulates are height, height texture (SD, variance), intensity, echo counts, waveform attributes. Urban only; grass appears as one of the ground classes, never as the object of study.

**Bartels, M., Wei, H. (2006). Segmentation of LIDAR data using measures of distribution. ISPRS Archives XXXVI-7 (Enschede). / Bartels, M., Wei, H., Mason, D.C. (2006). DTM generation from LIDAR data using skewness balancing. 18th Int. Conf. Pattern Recognition (ICPR), 566-569. DOI 10.1109/icpr.2006.463** [FULL for the ISPRS version; ICPR abstract via Reading CentAUR] Verbatim: "Naturally measured samples will lead to a normal distribution ... object points may disturb the normal distribution, and by removing those from the raw point cloud, normal distribution is achieved." The algorithm removes the highest point until the SKEWNESS of the remaining cloud is zero (kurtosis then converges to about 3; Figure 3 of the paper, 1,665 iterations on a 75,051-point tile with buildings and vegetation). Table 1 is the only place in this literature that writes the moments as a diagnostic (sk > 0, ku > 3: object dominance; sk = 0, ku = 3: normal ground). Later versions: Bartels & Wei 2010 Pattern Recognition Letters ("Threshold-free object and ground point separation"). Relevance: the one explicit use of the third and fourth moments of a return-height distribution in classification, and its premise is that GROUND IS GAUSSIAN and anything non-Gaussian is an object. The Elba sward cell, with exponential flanks and kurtosis 3.3, would be read by this rule as carrying objects.

### B. The Vienna full-waveform cluster: echo ratio, echo width, sigma-z roughness as cover features

**Rutzinger, M., Hofle, B., Hollaus, M., Pfeifer, N. (2008). Object-based point cloud analysis of full-waveform airborne laser scanning data for urban vegetation classification. Sensors 8(8), 4505-4528. DOI 10.3390/s8084505** [FULL; mdpi-res PDF] Vienna city center (Burggarten, Volksgarten), Riegl LMS-Q560, 1.5 um, 4 ns pulse, 0.5 mrad, 500 m AGL, 25 cm footprint, 15-20 emitted pulses/m2, winter 2006/07 leaf-off. Point-wise features: amplitude, echo width, echo number and count, and a ROUGHNESS defined as the SD of the plane-fitting residuals in a 3D neighborhood (verbatim: "building walls and inclined roof faces have a high SD of z-values, the here defined roughness will characterize them as smooth"). Region growing seeded by roughness and grown on echo-width homogeneity (1 ns tolerance scaled by the seed width); segment statistics (mean amplitude, mean echo width, roughness, echo count) feed a rule base / classification tree. Completeness and correctness > 90 % on two validation sites (all classification-tree settings over 90 % except amplitude-only, 84.07 %); compared with Ducic et al. 2006 (88.6 %). Low vegetation is excluded first "by a threshold on normalized heights" (verbatim, p. 143 of the extracted text), i.e. the sward is not a class. Relevance: the Vienna definition of per-cell roughness as a detrended SD is what the Elba NMAD measures; the paper's own diagnostic for vegetation is "geometrical inhomogeneity, which can be parameterized by for example roughness or a point density ratio measure", the two features Elba's sward core (NMAD 3-4 cm, 1-4 % penetration) sits at the floor of.

**Hofle, B., Hollaus, M. (2010). Urban vegetation detection using high density full-waveform airborne lidar data: combination of object-based image and point cloud analysis. ISPRS Archives XXXVIII-7B (Vienna), 281-286.** [FULL; isprs.org PDF] Whole-city Vienna FWF data, ~50 pts/m2, winter 2006/07; echo mix 6.8 % first, 1 % intermediate, 6.8 % last, 85.4 % single. The ECHO RATIO is given explicitly: ER [%] = (n_first + n_intermediate) / (n_last + n_single) x 100, set to 100 where multiple echoes occur within a cell; seed rules nDSM > 1.0 m and ER > 5 %; segment features include percent of echoes above 2 m (> 99 %), ER of segment (< 5 % excludes), mean echo width, SD of first-echo amplitude, compactness. 95.7 % of 668 alley trees detected; at most 14 % of vegetation segments confuse with buildings. Relevance: the published echo-ratio definition for 1,064/1,550 nm discrete echoes; in an Elba sward cell with 1-4 % of pulses yielding a second echo the ER is 1-4 %, below the 5 % seed threshold, i.e. "not vegetation" to this rule.

### G. The higher-moment strand: skewness and kurtosis of per-cell height and intensity distributions as classifiers

Verbatim reading: tall classes "tend to be bimodal with modes at canopy and ground level"; for short vegetation "the laser pulse distributions from ground to canopy tend to display a single strong mode with an otherwise irregular distribution. ## Synthesis

**Which per-cell return-shape features have separated which non-forest covers.** From Charaniya 2004 to Koma 2021 the separating features are the SECOND moment of the height distribution (height variation, variance, sigma-z, SD of plane residuals), the ZEROTH-moment counts (echo ratio, pulse penetration ratio, dropouts, layer densities) and INTENSITY. Grass is a class in Charaniya 2004 (intensity against roads, height variation against trees), Chehata 2009 (the worst-handled class, confused with ground where the beam reaches the soil), Brennan & Webster 2006 (stressed vs lush ground cover, 94 %), Antonarakis 2008 (short vegetation and bare earth > 90 %, with per-cell skewness and kurtosis of first and last pulses among the features), Koma 2021 (grassland vs reedbed vs shrub, 71 %, by height and variability) and Hollaus 2011 (grassland as the smooth end-member, 0.4-1.9 cm SD, read as "measurement noise"). Bare soil is an RMS roughness (Turner 2014, airborne) or, at TLS range, separated from residue by intensity (Koenig 2013). Water is a dropout-plus-intensity class (Hofle 2009). Gravel is the one surface whose detrended elevation distribution is written per cell as a full moment set (Brasington 2012; Hodge 2009b), at TLS density on bare sediment.

**Has anyone used the tails or flanks as a signature?** No. Higher moments appear in two roles only: a Gaussian-ground STOPPING RULE (Bartels 2006: strip points until skewness is 0 and kurtosis 3; Crosilla 2011/2013, Bao, Liu: the same on elevation and intensity), where anything non-Gaussian is "object"; and uninterpreted random-forest inputs labeled "vertical distribution of vegetation" (laserchicken kurto_, skew_; Koma 2021). Mallet 2011 shows within-pulse echo shape does not separate cover classes. The one printed set of per-class return-height distributions from wetland grass to forest (Hopkinson 2004, Figure 1) is percentile-normalized, described as "a single strong mode with an otherwise irregular distribution", and reduced to its SD. No paper plots a log-density histogram over a sward or bare field, fits a family, or names an exponential or power-law flank.

**Not in the literature relative to Elba.** (1) A sward-cell distribution below 0.5 m resolved to its flank slope at airborne density; the published grass floor is 2-5 cm SD. (2) The sub-sward SHOULDER as a feature: echo and penetration ratios register it as 1-4 %, below the ER > 5 % that defines "vegetation" in Vienna, and nobody reads the minority population's DEPTH. (3) Cover-dependent TAIL FORM (exponential under trees, steeper in open sward, power-law on bare ground); the moment strand assumes Gaussian ground and the metric catalogs do not interpret their skewness and kurtosis rasters. (4) Intensity as a function of depth within the distribution. (5) A 0.2-1 m tree-line layer read from a floodplain mosaic (Hollaus 2011's TR I layer is the container). The nearest instruments, the Laserfarm skew_/kurto_ rasters and Antonarakis 2008's per-cell first/last-pulse moments, have not been turned around to ask what a grass cell's shape says about the grass or the soil beneath it.
