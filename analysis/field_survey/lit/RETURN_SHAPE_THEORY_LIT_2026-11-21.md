# What shape should a lidar return-height distribution take? Theory and measurement (2026-11-21)

Agent literature search for Andy Wickert, lidar-diff-icp field-survey strand. Provenance tags:

## Question

Measured this week on the Elba floodplain sward (Whitewater River, MN) with the 3DEP May 2021
cloud (Leica TerrainMapper, 1,064 nm, ~17 pulses/m^2, 0.5 m footprint at 2,000 m AGL, 14-bit
peak-amplitude intensity, 0.5 m vendor minimum return separation): relative to the official 1 m
DEM, which IS the sward top, the stacked distribution of ~840,000 returns is a sharp peak whose
flanks are STRAIGHT on a log-density plot (an exponential core, e-folding 1.5-2 cm), with a
sub-surface population (the soil, 12-14 cm below the surface by RTK) that shows only as a
shoulder where the log-slope hands over from ~30 to ~7.5 decades/m, and a slow exponential tail
below the soil (e-folding 3-6 cm). Free two-Gaussian mixtures fail (the second Gaussian copies
the exponential flanks); subtracting a core fitted to the soil-free upper flank recovers the soil
to ~3 cm. Return intensity falls monotonically with depth below the surface.

Andy asks: what SHAPE should lidar return-height distributions take, according to published
theory and measurement? Five topics:

1. Single flat surface: the ranging-error distribution of a discrete-return system (pulse shape,
   timing jitter, detector type, range walk and its magnitude; footprint on a sloped or rough
   surface; speckle; measured histograms from hard flat surfaces).
2. Two surfaces within one pulse: peak position and amplitude of two overlapping echoes versus
   separation and amplitude ratio; minimum resolvable separation; bias of the merged peak.
3. Vegetation layer over ground: the expected height distribution (Beer-Lambert / MacArthur-Horn,
   Weibull/beta/gamma profiles, mixture models fitted to height histograms; predicted tail
   shapes).
4. Published histograms of returns on short vegetation relative to a reference surface.
5. Point-minus-DEM residual distributions for ground-classified points (robust interpolation,
   non-Gaussian / heavy-tailed statements).

## Findings by topic

### 1. Single flat surface: the ranging-error distribution

**What the detector does to one echo (derived, with formulas).**

**Thiel, K.-H., Wehr, A. (2004). Performance capabilities of laser scanners: an overview and measurement principle analysis. Int. Arch. Photogramm. Remote Sens. Spatial Inf. Sci. XXXVI-8/W2, 14-18.** [FULL; isprs.org; no DOI] Verbatim on fixed-threshold detection: "this detection process is very dependent on the reflectivity of the target. If the return signal becomes weaker the threshold level will be passed later ... This means, a too long travelling time of the laser signal is measured and by that a too long slant range. To compensate this intensity dependent effect, the level of the return signal must be determined and a correction is applied to the measuring result. A possible one correction t_k is t_k = t_r P_thr / P_peak, if P_thr is the threshold level, P_peak the peak level and t_r the rise time." The constant-fraction discriminator "relax[es] the dependency on the level of the pulse return signal." Ranging precision for pulse systems (their eq. 3, attributed to Bachman 1979): sigma_R = (c / 4 pi) T_P / (S/N), with T_P the pulse width; conclusion verbatim: "Only systems with fixed threshold produce systematic offset with decreasing reflectivity." Relevance: this is the published range-walk law in closed form. MY ARITHMETIC from it, inputs stated: a ~4 ns FWHM system pulse has a 10-90 % rise time of roughly 2-2.5 ns; a threshold at 5 % of a strong echo's peak gives t_k = 0.1 ns = 1.5 cm, at 50 % of a weak echo's peak 1.0-1.25 ns = 15-19 cm. So a fixed-threshold detector moves the range by 1-20 cm, one-sided (weak echoes read LONG, i.e. LOW), over the amplitude range a sward presents. Whether the TerrainMapper uses a fixed threshold, a constant-fraction trigger or a peak/correlation estimator is NOT public (dead end above), so the walk's presence in the 2021 data is a hypothesis, not a fact. <!-- spelling: ok (verbatim "travelling") -->

**Wagner, W., Ullrich, A., Melzer, T., Briese, C., Kraus, K. (2004). From single-pulse to full-waveform airborne laser scanners: potential and practical challenges. Int. Arch. XXXV-B3, 201-206.** [FULL; already cited in the repo for the 5-10 ns pulse and the merged wheat waveform; the per-detector numbers are new here] Five detectors (threshold, center of gravity, maximum, zero crossing of the second derivative, constant fraction) applied to simulated waveforms from a "10 ns" Q-switched pulse. Table 1 (range errors in m, true distance in parentheses): single Gaussian target at 9.0 m, threshold +0.045, the three difference-based detectors exact; wheat over ground 0.6 m apart (the merged case), crops at 8.4 m: zero crossing +0.015, threshold +0.135, constant fraction -0.030, maximum and center of gravity return nothing for the crop; ground at 9.0 m: zero crossing +0.045, maximum +0.150, center of gravity +0.240; 45-degree roof with a 1 m footprint: zero crossing +0.075, maximum -0.150, threshold +0.240, center of gravity -0.135, constant fraction +0.015, "the range values obtained by using different detectors may vary by ~0.4 m ... given that the laser footprint is 1 m." The LMS-Q560 "single target measurement accuracy is about 2 cm (1 sigma value)." "Maximum, zero crossing, and constant fraction are invariant with respect to amplitude variations." Relevance: the detector choice alone moves a merged ground-plus-low-vegetation range by 0.1-0.25 m; amplitude-invariant detectors exist and are standard; the single-target 1 sigma of a 2004 full-waveform instrument was 2 cm.

**Jutzi, B., Stilla, U. (2005). Measuring and processing the waveform of laser pulses. Optical 3-D Measurement Techniques VII (Vienna), 10 pp.** [FULL; pia.tum.de] and **Jutzi, B., Stilla, U. (2006). Range determination with waveform recording laser systems using a Wiener Filter. ISPRS J. Photogramm. Remote Sens. 61(2), 95-107. doi:10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2006.09.001** [ABS; Helava award paper; Crossref-verified] plus **Jutzi 2007, DGK Reihe C Heft 611 (dissertation, German)** [FULL text, abstract read]. The 2005 paper measures the same urban scene twice with a 20 GSample/s experimental receiver from a 15 m platform (ranges to 200 m) and histograms the per-pulse range DIFFERENCES by detector (their Fig. 3 a-f; Table 1 standard deviations in m): peak 0.200, leading edge (half-maximum threshold) 0.190, constant fraction 0.223, center of gravity 0.190, Levenberg-Marquardt Gaussian fit 0.185, correlation 0.173; "the average value of difference in range for all investigated algorithms is below 1 cm"; "the poorest results were reached by using a single value (peak, leading edge, constant fraction)." They also note that "the transmitted waveform differs strongly from well-known models (Gaussian, exponential, or rectangular function)" pulse to pulse. The dissertation abstract: the Wiener-filtered surface response "demonstrates a discrimination of two areas, with a range difference of 0.15 m for a typical pulse length of 5 ns." Relevance: (a) these are the only published HISTOGRAMS of single-echo range repeatability by detector that this search found, and they are reported as standard deviations only, not fitted to a family; (b) 0.15 m two-surface discrimination needs full-waveform deconvolution, and even then it is the same number as the Elba sward depth.

**Amann, M.-C., Bosch, T., Lescure, M., Myllyla, R., Rioux, M. (2001). Laser ranging: a critical review of usual techniques for distance measurement. Optical Engineering 40(1), 10-19. doi:10.1117/1.1330700** [ABS; Crossref carries "unusual" in the title, a known metadata error] The standard engineering review of time-of-flight timing error (jitter from noise on the leading edge, walk from amplitude). Cited here for provenance only; its formulas were not read. The University of Oulu rangefinder theses reached by the same search report walk error below +-1 mm over a 1:10 amplitude range with a high-bandwidth receiver, i.e. the walk is an engineering choice, not a physical floor.

**Gardner, C.S. (1992). Ranging performance of satellite laser altimeters. IEEE Trans. Geosci. Remote Sens. 30(5), 1061-1072. doi:10.1109/36.175341** [FULL via the NASA NTRS copy of the University of Illinois report UILU-ENG-92-2547; the repo had the DOI only] and **Gardner, C.S. (1982). Target signatures for laser altimeters: an analysis. Applied Optics 21(3), 448-458. doi:10.1364/ao.21.000448** [ABS; Crossref-verified]. The 1982 abstract: expressions for the mean and temporal covariance of the received pulse "accounting for laser speckle, shot noise, and the target's surface profile"; the search-engine summary of its result, "for short pulses, small nadir angles, and small divergence, the expected received pulse shape is identical to the surface profile probability density function," is CITED-IN only (not read in the paper). The 1992 report, verbatim: "Since the ground target is a diffuse reflector, the statistics of the reflected signal are identical to statistics for fully developed speckle. The signal amplitude is a circular complex Gaussian process and the signal intensity obeys negative exponential statistics"; the speckle signal-to-noise ratio K_s (receiver area over speckle correlation area) "is on the order of 10^4-10^5," so for a large receiver aperture speckle is a negligible contributor to the pulse-energy variance; "The most effective technique for estimating range when the pulse shape is unknown is to compute the centroid of the received pulse. The performance of this estimator is proportional to the rms pulse width and is inversely proportional to the square root of the signal photon count." The surface is modeled as z(x,y) = x tan S_par + y tan S_perp + dz(x,y), i.e. a plane plus "quasi-random surface roughness variations." Equation (18), the rms width of the received pulse, is a sum in quadrature of four terms: (sigma_t^2 + sigma_h^2) [transmit pulse and receiver impulse response], 4 Var(dz) cos^2 S_par / (c^2 cos^2(phi + S_par)) [surface roughness], a beam-curvature term, and a nadir-angle-and-slope term proportional to z^2 tan^2 theta_T [tan^2 theta_T + tan^2(phi + S_par) + tan^2 S_perp / ...]. Relevance: THIS is the derivation behind "the received waveform is the transmit pulse convolved with the footprint's height distribution"; a within-footprint roughness of sigma_dz adds 2 sigma_dz / c to the rms width in quadrature (a 2 cm sward-top roughness is 0.13 ns against a ~1.7 ns rms system pulse, i.e. invisible in width); and the model is Gaussian in all its terms, so it predicts a Gaussian-cored, not exponential-flanked, population from a flat surface.

**Abshire, J.B., et al. (2005). Geoscience Laser Altimeter System (GLAS) on the ICESat mission: on-orbit measurement performance. Geophys. Res. Lett. 32, L21S02. doi:10.1029/2005GL024028** [ABS; citing NASA papers read]: 6 ns pulse "preserved" on flat bright ice; receiver ranging precision "2-3 cm standard deviation" throughout the mission (from a 2013-era NASA receiver study). Relevance: a centroid-type waveform ranger on a flat target reaches 2-3 cm 1 sigma at a 6 ns pulse; a 4 ns discrete system should do no worse in the ideal case, which is the ~1 cm floor the repo measured on built surfaces (memory `instrumental-precision.md`).

**Measured width of the single-surface population, discrete-return airborne data.**

**Davenport, I.J., Holden, N., Gurney, R.J. (2004). Characterizing errors in airborne laser altimetry data to extract soil roughness. IEEE Trans. Geosci. Remote Sens. 42(10), 2130-2141. doi:10.1109/tgrs.2004.834648** [FULL; Reading CentAUR accepted manuscript] Optech ALTM 1205 (0.3 mrad, footprint ~11 cm at 370 m) over a 120 x 120 m field "ploughed, harrowed with a rotary cultivator, then rolled flat" to within 1 cm per meter, 41,384 comparisons against 408 ground points over 37 minutes; a second campaign with an ALTM 2033 (28 swaths over 3.5 hours, 0.3 and 1.0 mrad, footprints 19-22 cm and 67-69 cm). Three noise sources, verbatim: "(1) The internal noise inherent in every measurement, due to the timing electronics and the Inertial Navigation System (INS) corrections, in this case with a standard deviation of 3.5cm. We refer to this later as the point timing noise. (2) The random noise from individual DGPS height measurements ... with a standard deviation of 2.1cm. Combined with the timing noise this makes a total standard deviation of 4.1cm. (3) The drift of the measured height over periods of a few minutes ... about 7cm over 37 minutes." The temporal semivariogram has a nugget of 0.0006 m^2 at 50 ms lag. The distribution is ASSUMED normal: "This imparts a normal distribution noise with a standard deviation of 4.1cm, or 3.5cm for points closer than 50ms"; "With a normal distribution, 99.7% of measurements will occur within three standard deviations." Swath-to-swath offsets on a flat lab roof: mean 7.8 cm, maximum 30.2 cm. The roughness method: "In height measurements of a featureless, flat, level surface, the standard deviation of returns would be a measurement of the height variation due to the instrument error"; detrended SD of returns over rough soil = instrument noise plus surface variability. Relevance: the one airborne paper built around a prepared flat soil surface; it gives the single-surface width (3.5 cm, 2002-era ALTM) and the drift, treats the shape as Gaussian by assumption, and prints offset distributions (its Figs. 5 and 8) only as swath means. No histogram of individual returns about the flat surface is shown with a fitted shape. <!-- spelling: ok (verbatim "ploughed") -->

**van der Sande, C., Soudarissanane, S., Khoshelham, K. (2010). Assessment of relative accuracy of AHN-2 laser scanning data using planar features. Sensors 10(9), 8198-8214. doi:10.3390/s100908198** [FULL, skimmed; mdpi-res PDF] Point-to-plane residuals of overlapping AHN-2 strips on planar features: "Before the adjustment the standard deviations range from 3 cm to 11 cm. **Hohle, J., Hohle, M. (2009). Accuracy assessment of digital elevation models by means of robust statistical methods. ISPRS J. Photogramm. Remote Sens. 64(4), 398-406. doi:10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2009.02.003** [FULL; Aalborg VBN publisher PDF; the repo had metadata only] The paper that made the field non-parametric says WHY, verbatim, under their Fig. 1 ("Histogram of the errors dh in metres ... The latter effect is measured by the kurtosis of the distribution, which in this situation is bigger than zero." Relevance for topic 1: "more peaked around its mean ... while having heavier tails" is the verbal description of a Laplace-like (exponential-flanked) error distribution, stated for DEM checkpoint errors (their example is photogrammetric), and it is the closest any standard-setting paper comes to naming the shape Andy measured; they stop at excess kurtosis > 0 and do not fit a family. <!-- spelling: ok (verbatim "metres") -->

**Where an exponential-flanked single-surface core COULD come from (statistics, not lidar literature).** **Andrews, D.F., Mallows, C.L. (1974). Scale mixtures of normal distributions. J. Royal Statistical Society B 36(1), 99-102. doi:10.1111/j.2517-6161.1974.tb00989.x** [ABS; Crossref-verified; the "Gauss-Laplace transmutation" note arXiv 1510.08765 restates it]: a zero-mean normal whose VARIANCE is exponentially distributed is exactly Laplace (double exponential); more generally a Gamma-distributed precision gives Student-t, and any broad scale mixture of normals is "more peaked ... with heavier tails" in Hohle's words. Combined with Thiel & Wehr's eq. (3), sigma_R proportional to 1/(S/N), and Gardner's centroid result, sigma proportional to 1/sqrt(N): if the per-pulse echo energy varies widely across a sward (it does; the stacked intensity falls monotonically with depth in Andy's data), the pooled ranging error is a scale mixture of Gaussians and its flanks are heavier than Gaussian, exactly exponential only for an exponential variance law. This bridge is MINE; no lidar paper found derives the pooled return-height distribution as a scale mixture, and no paper reports Laplace flanks on a single hard surface (Hejmanowska & Kay 2011's Laplace fit, in the repo memory, is on pooled mixed-cover DTM error, not on one surface). Treat as a testable hypothesis: bin the 2021 returns by intensity and test whether each intensity class is Gaussian with its own sigma while the pool is exponential-flanked. The premise that the range variance depends on intensity is measured directly for terrestrial scanners by **AbdelGafar, O., Palaz, S., Yang, Y., Holst, C. (2025). General model for estimating range variances of terrestrial laser scanners based on (un-)scaled intensity values. arXiv 2510.25587** [ABS]: "the range variance varies with different intensity measurements," modeled from raw (Z+F Imager 5016A) and scaled (Leica ScanStation P50) intensities.

### 2. Two surfaces within one pulse: merged echoes

**Thiel & Wehr 2004** [FULL, as above], Fig. 9 "Effect of a Second Height Level within the Laser Spot on Ranging Performance", verbatim: "The offset between ground and roof is held constant at 6 m and the fraction between ground and roof in the backscattered signal varies from zero to 100 percent. The phase measurement shows a continuous range changing from ground to roof ... However, the pulse system exhibits a more complex ranging behavior which can strongly influence the ranging result. For a constant threshold system the range measurement remains at ground level value up to a ratio of 30% roof and 70% ground. There is only a small increase in percentage when using a filter. A correlation detection using maximum correlation signal detection ... jumps at 50% from ground range to roof range." Also: "Multiple height levels within the laser spot can be resolved best by special correlation method, where height levels below the pulse width can be detected" (their Fig. 8 resolves steps of 2.5 and 3.5 m at 500 m range). Relevance: the ONLY published curve found of detected range versus energy split for two targets in one footprint; at a separation (6 m) much larger than the pulse the detected range is a STEP (bistable) in the split, with the switch point set by the detector (30 % for threshold, 50 % for peak-correlation). For a separation much SMALLER than the pulse the step becomes a ramp (next item).

**Hofton, M.A., Minster, J.B., Blair, J.B. (2000). Decomposition of laser altimeter waveforms. IEEE Trans. Geosci. Remote Sens. 38(4), 1989-1996. doi:10.1109/36.851780** [FULL; NASA NTRS preprint] The resolvability rule in Gaussian decomposition, verbatim: "We use the fact that a single Gaussian has two inflection points and that when n Gaussians are combined there will be 2n inflection points at most. Difficulties arise when two neighboring Gaussians are close enough together that only two inflection points (instead of four) are detected, making it impossible to isolate the close Gaussian pair." Components with initial half-width below the system impulse response or amplitude below 3 sigma of the noise are not ranked "important." Relevance: a 13 cm pair under a 0.6 m pulse produces two inflection points, i.e. one component, in any decomposition; the merged pair is unresolvable in principle, not for lack of sampling.

**Wagner et al. 2004** [FULL, as above]: wheat 0.6 m above ground, "their vertical profiles are merged into a unimodal waveform by the convolution"; only the zero-crossing detector "generates two trigger-pulses and correctly resolves (discriminates) the waveform into ground and vegetation components," and it "is sensitive to noise"; threshold on the merged crop echo reads +0.135 m long, center of gravity on the ground echo +0.240 m. Relevance: with a 0.6 m separation, i.e. exactly one FWHM, the merged waveform is already unimodal and the detected range depends on the detector by up to 0.24 m.

**Jutzi 2007 dissertation / Jutzi & Stilla 2006** [FULL abstract; ABS]: two areas 0.15 m apart are discriminated for a 5 ns pulse only after Wiener-filter deconvolution of the recorded waveform; **Neilsen, K.D. (2011). Signal processing on digitized ladar waveforms for enhanced resolution on surface edges. MS thesis, Utah State University** [FULL, abstract and contents; gray literature]: "Using deconvolution on the received waveform, an increase from 30 cm to 14 cm in range resolution is reported. Error on these measurements has a 2 cm standard deviation." Relevance: 13-15 cm is the documented floor of two-surface separation with FULL waveforms and deconvolution; a discrete-return instrument with a 0.5 m minimum separation (TerrainMapper spec, in GROUND_UNDER_LOW_VEG) is three to four times coarser.

**Ullrich & Pfennigbauer 2011, Parrish et al. 2011, Hug 2004, Ussyshkin & Theriault 2011** are already in the repo (multi-target resolution set by the system pulse width; dead zones 0.6-3.5 m). Roncat, Bergauer & Pfeifer 2011 (ISPRS J. 66, 418-428, B-spline deconvolution for the differential cross-section) [record level only] and Roncat's 2014 TU Wien dissertation belong to the same deconvolution family and were not read.

**The merged-peak algebra for the Elba case (MY CALCULATION, run 2026-11-21 in the scratchpad, inputs stated).** Two Gaussian echoes of equal width (system FWHM 0.6 m, i.e. 4 ns; also 0.45 and 0.3 m), the upper at the sward top z = 0 and the lower at the soil z = -0.13 m, with a fraction f of the echo energy in the upper one. The sum has ONE maximum for every f at all three widths, and its position is z_peak = -0.13 (1 - f) to within 3 mm at 0.6 m FWHM (within 1 cm at 0.3 m): a LINEAR ramp from the top to the soil as the soil's share rises. Two maxima at equal amplitude first appear at a separation of 2.0 sigma = 0.85 FWHM, which is 0.51 m for a 0.6 m FWHM pulse; this reproduces the vendor's 0.5 m minimum return separation from the pulse width alone. Consequences stated as derivation, not citation: (1) a peak-detected discrete return from a 13 cm sward over soil is the energy-weighted mean of the two surfaces, so the stacked return-height distribution is the distribution of the soil's energy share f, stretched over 0.13 m, convolved with the single-surface ranging error; (2) a SECOND MODE at the soil requires pulses whose energy is almost entirely soil (f near 0, i.e. footprints landing in gaps larger than the 0.5 m footprint); a continuum of partial gap fractions gives a one-sided FLANK or SHOULDER running from the top toward the soil and no mode there; (3) a fixed-threshold detector would instead hold the range near the brighter surface until the split passes a detector-set fraction (Thiel & Wehr's 30 %), then jump, which would produce two clusters, not a ramp. Andy's shoulder, not a second mode, is what the peak/centroid branch predicts; it argues against a fixed-threshold detector in the TerrainMapper, which is otherwise undocumented. For completeness: **Rife, J.H., Li, Y. (2026). Characterizing lidar range-measurement ambiguity due to multiple returns. arXiv 2604.09282** [ABS] treats the same two-surfaces-in-one-beam case for automotive scanners empirically, as per-ray cumulative distributions of the returned range, i.e. as a probabilistic choice between the surfaces rather than a ramp; it is the only 2026 paper found on the subject and it is not airborne.

## Synthesis

**Single surface.** Theory predicts a Gaussian core: the echo is the transmit pulse convolved with the footprint's height distribution, its rms width a quadrature sum of pulse, receiver, roughness and slope terms (Gardner 1982, 1992), and the range scatter scales as pulse width over signal-to-noise (Thiel & Wehr 2004; Gardner's centroid), speckle negligible. Measured widths: 2 cm 1 sigma (Wagner 2004), 3.5 cm on rolled soil (Davenport 2004), 2-5 cm planar residuals (van der Sande 2010), 2-3 cm (GLAS), 6 cm RMSDv as the 3DEP acceptance line, 1.1 cm NMAD in this repo. None prints the shape or fits a family; Davenport assumes normality, Hohle & Hohle 2009 say "more peaked ... with heavier tails" and stop at kurtosis > 0. A fixed-threshold detector adds a one-sided walk of t_r P_thr/P_peak, 1-20 cm for a 4 ns pulse over a sward's amplitude range (my arithmetic from Thiel's formula); constant-fraction, peak and zero-crossing detectors are amplitude-invariant (Wagner 2004); the TerrainMapper's detector is not public.

**Merged echoes for a 13 cm sward under a 0.5 m pulse.** Hofton 2000: components closer than the pulse give two inflection points and are unresolvable; Wagner 2004: 0.6 m apart is already unimodal, detector-dependent by 0.24 m; Thiel & Wehr 2004, the only published range-versus-energy-split curve: at large separation the range is bistable, switching at 30 % (threshold) or 50 % (peak). My two-Gaussian calculation for 0.13 m under a 0.3-0.6 m FWHM pulse: one maximum for every split, at -0.13 (1 - f) within 3 mm, and two maxima only beyond 0.85 FWHM = 0.51 m, the vendor's spec. A peak-type ranger therefore reads the energy-weighted mean of sward and soil, and the stacked distribution is the distribution of the soil's energy share stretched over 13 cm, convolved with the single-surface error: a ramp or SHOULDER toward the soil, and a soil MODE only where whole footprints hit bare ground. Hopkinson 2004, Straatsma 2007 (inflection ~5 cm) and Rogers 2018 describe one mode for vegetation thinner than the pulse.

**Why exponential flanks.** No lidar source predicts them. Three routes, by support: (1) a scale mixture of Gaussians, exactly Laplace for an exponentially distributed variance (Andrews & Mallows 1974), supplied by the SNR-dependent sigma and the measured intensity fall-off with depth (AbdelGafar 2025 measures sigma(intensity) for TLS); (2) a continuum of energy splits with a near-exponential distribution, unpublished; (3) range walk, one-sided, so it cannot give two straight flanks alone. Microrelief adds width in quadrature (Gardner) or skew (BepiColombo 2026), not exponential flanks.

**Not published:** a fitted family for single-surface airborne ranging error; a log-density return-height histogram over any sward; the merged-echo ramp as a model of a stacked height distribution; any lower-tail (sub-soil) population; Jaskierniak 2011's eleven surviving families (abstract only).
