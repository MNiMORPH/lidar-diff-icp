# Recovering the true ground surface under low vegetation from airborne lidar: bias and correction literature (2026-11-20)

## Question

Elba floodplain hay meadow (nettle, grass, goldenrod), Whitewater River, MN. Every return of the
May 2021 3DEP flight (1,064 nm, ~17 pulses/m^2; 98 % of pulses give one return and no two returns
of one pulse are closer than 0.63 m) sits 0.13 m above RTK ground with a 3-4 cm spread; only 1-4 %
of pulses reach the soil; the lowest return in a 5 m window sits a median 5 cm above the soil; the
vendor's class-2 ground IS the sward surface; the vendor's held-out checkpoints read -0.008 m
(bare) and -0.09 m (vegetated). The November 2008 flight (~1 pulse/m^2) sat 0.2-0.4 m up and never
reached the soil. Andy asks what has been done to RECOVER the true ground where the returns come
from low vegetation:

1. vegetation-induced DEM bias by cover (grass, marsh, crop, shrub, tundra) and its magnitude;
2. correction methods: cover-specific survey offsets, NDVI regression (LEAN and successors),
   minimum / low-percentile / morphological filters, full-waveform decomposition for the weak
   ground echo under a short canopy, multi-temporal or leaf-off flights, green and high-density
   UAV lidar, and the probability-of-penetration / lowest-return statistics;
3. the sensor fact that single-return airborne lidar with a ~0.5 m footprint cannot separate a
   13 cm sward from the soil (dead time, pulse width, minimum return separation);
4. 3DEP / USGS Lidar Base Specification statements on vegetated vertical accuracy (VVA).

Another agent covers the ECOLOGICAL use of the offset; this note covers ground recovery and bias.

### A. Bias by cover: upland grass, forb meadow, shrub, dune grass, tundra (what the marsh notes do not hold)

**Hodgson, M.E., Bresnahan, P. (2004). Accuracy of airborne lidar-derived elevation: empirical assessment and error budget. Photogrammetric Engineering & Remote Sensing 70(3), 331-339. DOI 10.14358/PERS.70.3.331** [ABS; Crossref-confirmed DOI; full PDF not reached, scholarcommons returned HTML] 650 checkpoints in six classes (pavement, low grass, high grass, brush/low trees, evergreen, deciduous). RMSE 17-19 cm (pavement, low grass, evergreen) to 26 cm (deciduous); error at 25 deg slope about twice that at 1.5 deg. Gopfert & Heipke 2006 [FULL] quote the SIGNED shifts as -4.6 cm (evergreen) and +1.0 cm (deciduous). The high-grass value was not captured in any snippet; it is in the paper's table. Relevance: the one early study with a HIGH GRASS (to 90 cm) class in the upland US; the signed high-grass number is the thing to pull from the table when the PDF is reached.

**Hodgson, M.E., Jensen, J., Raber, G., Tullis, J., Davis, B.A., Schuckman, K., Thompson, G. (2005). An evaluation of lidar-derived elevation and terrain slope in leaf-off conditions. PE&RS 71(7), 817-823.** [ABS; DOI not retrieved] Winter leaf-off flight, NC piedmont, 1,225 survey-grade points, six classes. RMSE 14.5-36.1 cm, largest in scrub-shrub; "the lidar-derived elevation under-predicted true elevation regardless of land cover category," to -23.6 cm under pine; low grass error rises consistently with slope over 0-10 deg. Relevance: a leaf-off flight whose SIGN is negative everywhere (lidar LOW), the opposite of every herbaceous-marsh study, which the authors do not explain; it bounds how far a vendor level offset can masquerade as a cover effect.

**Tinkham, W.T., Huang, H., Smith, A.M.S., Shrestha, R., Falkowski, M.J., Hudak, A.T., Link, T.E., Glenn, N.F., Marks, D.G. (2011). A comparison of two open source LiDAR surface classification algorithms. Remote Sensing 3(3), 638-649. DOI 10.3390/rs3030638** [FULL; USFS Treesearch PDF] Reynolds Creek, Idaho; Leica ALS50 Phase II, 1,064 nm, mid-November 2007, 6 pulses/m2 nominal (5.7 returns/m2 mean), four returns per pulse; vendor RTK on asphalt RMSE 3.3 cm; >7,000 survey points. The meadow "is a mixture of forbs and graminoids with Lupinus ssp. and Carex ssp. dominating the forbs and Poa ssp. dominating the graminoids." Table 3 (meters, survey minus lidar sign not stated in the table; the text calls these "mean error"): Forb -0.058 (sd 0.116) with BCAL and -0.061 (sd 0.116) with MCC; Shrub -0.052 (0.263) / -0.046 (0.265); Ceanothus -0.012 (0.125) / +0.342 (0.229); Aspen -0.151 / -0.149; Conifer -0.160 / -0.085; Overall -0.106 (0.252) / -0.079 (0.268). Also: "BCAL is able to create a more reliable surface in very dense, continuous vegetation like that which occurs in the ceanothus and willow cover types. This is most likely due to the block minimum approach that BCAL uses, allowing it to create a surface from fewer points than MCC." Relevance: (a) a November, leaf-off, 6 pulses/m2, 1,064 nm flight over a forb/sedge/Poa meadow, the closest acquisition analog to the 2008 Elba flight in season and sensor family (ALS50-II), gives a forb-meadow sd of 11.6 cm; (b) the paper reports the 0.13 m "forb meadow RMSE" that Tinkham 2012 cites (already in LIT_LOW_VEGETATION_LIDAR.md) and the two filters agree on it to 3 mm, i.e. the meadow error is NOT a filter artifact; (c) the block-minimum filter (BCAL) beats curvature (MCC) by 0.35 m under a dense low shrub, the clearest published case of a low order statistic recovering ground that a surface-fitting filter cannot.

**Gould, S.B., Glenn, N.F., Sankey, T.T., McNamara, J.P. (2013). Influence of a dense, low-height shrub species on the accuracy of a lidar-derived DEM. PE&RS 79(5), 421-431.** [FULL; Boise State PDF] Ceanothus velutinus (dense broadleaf evergreen shrub), SW Idaho; 4.09-8.68 points/m2, ~0.20 m footprint, 0.22 mrad divergence, vendor RMSE 0.026 m on 504 points; survey-grade GPS transects. With optimal BCAL parameters RMSE in ceanothus 0.17-0.26 m on slopes <25 deg and 0.28-0.37 m on slopes >=25 deg. The penetration count, verbatim: "Results found only 5 of the total (15,703) returns with elevations that were equal to the DEM_survey elevations and 149 returns with elevation difference <=0.15 m"; "Fewer than half of the ground classified returns in our study had elevations within 0.30 m of the DEM_survey elevations." The stated mechanism: "Few openings in the canopy are large enough for lidar pulses (~0.20 m diameter) to pass through"; and, citing Schmid 2011, in short vegetation the return "becomes comingled with the reflected energy from the ground surface, and the system is unable to discern more than one return." Relevance: the one paper that COUNTS pulses reaching the ground under a dense low canopy at airborne density: 5 of 15,703 (0.03 %) at the ground, 149 (0.95 %) within 15 cm. Elba's 1-4 % within 5 cm of the soil in a hay sward is the same order; Gould's conclusion that the ground-classified set is then dominated by mislabeled canopy returns is the mechanism behind "the vendor's class-2 IS the sward surface."

**Gopfert, J., Heipke, C. (2006). Assessment of lidar DTM accuracy in coastal vegetated areas. Int. Arch. Photogramm. Remote Sens. Spatial Inf. Sci. XXXVI part 3, 79-85.** [FULL; isprs.org P_10.pdf] <!-- spelling: ok (verbatim) --> East Frisian islands (Langeoog, Riegl scanner; Juist, Optech ALTM 2050, March 2004, 1,000 m AGL, 2 points/m2), surveyed control points per vegetation type with field vegetation height and fisheye-photo canopy cover. Types: Japanese rose (<1.3 m), beach grass Ammophila arenaria (<1.0 m), crowberry (<0.4 m), creeping willow (<1.6 m), sea buckthorn (<1.4 m), common reed (<2.2 m), sand couch grass Agropyron pungens (<0.5 m), two mixed habitats. Result, verbatim: "the lidar DTM is higher than the related control points for each vegetation type (8-24 cm) ... For each type the standard deviation of a single measurement is only in the range from 5 up to 15 cm. The highest height shift was detected for beach grass (+19.3 cm), seabuckthorn (+18.4 cm), sand couch grass (+20.1 cm) and the mixed area seabuckthorn/willow (+23.2 cm)." Correlation of the shift with fisheye canopy cover 0.19 over all types, "high" for beach grass and rose; with DoA 0.39 (beach grass) and NDVI 0.23 (dunes; "the measurement campaign was conducted in spring, the plants ... had just started their activity"); negative correlation with lidar intensity (-0.51 for beach grass). Method: a supervised classification of the lidar DSM, DSM height texture (co-occurrence contrast), intensity and CIR imagery into ACCURACY CLASSES (shift up to +15, +30, +45, +60, >+60 cm) rather than a continuous correction. The introduction collates the European low-vegetation shifts: Oude Elberink & Crombaghs 2004 "up to 15 cm for low vegetated areas (creeping red fescue)"; Ahokas et al. 2003 sd asphalt 10 cm, gravel 4, grass 11, forest ground 17 cm; Pfeifer et al. 2004 "long dense grass (+7.3 cm), young forest (+9.4 cm) and old willow forest (+11.6 cm)." **Gopfert & Soergel (2007). Estimation of the lidar height offset in coastal vegetated areas. ISPRS XXXVI-3/W52.** [FULL] Replaces the accuracy classes with continuous (polynomial / exponential) functions of height contrast and intensity fitted by least squares; "a general upwards shift of the lidar DTM ranging from 10 cm to 23 cm for several control areas"; Table 1 correlation of offset with height contrast 0.46-0.72 for willow; 2.9 points/m2. Relevance: a SAND COUCH GRASS at <0.5 m gives +20.1 cm, a 0.5 m sward reading as the top of itself, exactly Elba's 0.13 m on a ~0.15-0.3 m sward; and the correction they propose, DSM texture from the laser data alone, is a 2006 version of our `lowveg` fraction. The texture-only route reached only r = 0.4-0.7, and the 2007 paper still needed control plots per type.

**Pfeifer, N., Gorte, B., Oude Elberink, S. (2004). Influences of vegetation on laser altimetry: analysis and correction approaches. ISPRS XXXVI-8/W2, 283-287.** [FULL; isprs.org PFEIFER(2).pdf] Dutch test fields, 24 control-point areas triangulated to a TIN, laser points compared to it. Table 1 (mm, laser above control): long dense grass, all five areas, n = 8,453, mean +73.1, sd 84.1, min/max -248/+554 (per area +50.0 to +104.7); young forest n = 2,113, +94.2 (sd 101.7); old willow forest n = 1,216, +116.3 (sd 116.5). Cited there: Pfeifer, Stadler & Briese 2001 sd of DTM vs check points, street without cars +/-1.0 cm, open park grass +/-4.5 cm, dense trees +/-11.1 cm, with "a systematic shift of the DTM above the check points"; Ahokas 2003 sd asphalt 10, gravel 4, grass 11, forest ground 17 cm; a Dutch study (reference truncated) "an upward shift of 8 cm on long dense grass, and 4 cm on solid ground," sd rising "from +/-7 cm on solid ground to +/-14 cm on low vegetation"; Oude Elberink & Crombaghs 2004 "upward shifts occurred up to 15 cm on low vegetation ... 0 % coverage meant no upward shift, 100 % coverage showed a 15 cm shift." Mechanism, verbatim: "Footprint size ... is usually in the range of 50 cm to 1 m. This means that all objects within this area contribute to the backscatter. Over vegetation the returning echo is therefore a mixture of ground (i.e. soil) reflections and the leaves of the vegetation (e.g. grass). Because of this the measured range lies systematically above the terrain"; "The pulse width is usually a few nano seconds, with 1 ns corresponding to 30 cm of length. If two surfaces (e.g., upper side of a shrub and the ground) are within one footprint only two distinct echoes are generated, if the pulses are at least half the pulse width apart ... for the TopoSys laser scanner the 'two successive echoes must have a significant larger distance than the pulse duration of 5 ns' (!), corresponding to 1.5 m one-way travel distance." Correction: the vegetation-TYPE approach (a lookup table of offsets) is judged "not very practical" because the type must be mapped; the TEXTURE approach regresses the shift on point-cloud co-occurrence contrast in 50-nearest-neighbor environments, R2 = 0.09 (per point) and 0.36 (per field) in one design, 0.35 and 0.47 in the other.

**Gorte, B., Pfeifer, N., Oude Elberink, S. (2005). Height texture of low vegetation in airborne laser scanner data and its potential for DTM correction. ISPRS XXXVI-3/W19, 150.** [FULL; isprs.org] The follow-up on VERY LOW grass in flat salt-marsh fields ("wad") and dike fields in the Netherlands: 8 vegetation fields of 100-160 m2 surveyed by RTK-GPS (+/-2 cm) on 15 and 18 October 2004, Optech ALTM flown 11 October 2004, "overall point density is higher than 1 point/m2, but not homogeneous"; vegetation "heights below 20 cm, e.g. grass." Results, verbatim: in the fields "the average shift has a value between 10 and 15 cm" (several), "between 5 and 10 cm" (3), "between 0 and 5 cm" (4), "and in one field it is negative (-2.2 cm)"; between two strips a relative shift of -1.6 cm with sd 10 cm; per strip "a standard deviation of 7 cm: the combined effect of noise and texture." The central negative finding: "We conclude here that the spatial frequency of the texture (caused by vegetation) is too high to be captured by the laser point density. The plants are much smaller than the point spacing. Therefore, the influence of plants on measured heights is stochastic. Vegetation causes noise rather than spatial patterns"; "in the range of standard deviations between 2 cm and 6 cm and shift values between -5 cm and +10 cm no functional connection can be [established]"; the SD-to-height relation "successful for larger vegetation (30 cm or higher), it is not" for very low vegetation; the fallback offered is a flat rule, "If the standard deviation of the measurements is between 3 cm and 5 cm, a shift of 5 cm gives on average the best improvement." Relevance: this is the published test of the 2008 Elba situation (grass < 20-30 cm, ~1 pt/m2, October, RTK truth), and it FAILED to predict the shift from the laser's own texture; the shift is real (0-15 cm per field) but reads as noise at that density. It is also the sharpest statement in the literature that the ground-recovery problem at 1 pt/m2 over a sward is underdetermined from the cloud alone.

**Toyra, J., Pietroniro, A., Hopkinson, C., Kalbfleisch, W. (2003). Assessment of airborne scanning laser altimetry (lidar) in a deltaic wetland environment. Canadian J. Remote Sensing 29(6), 718-728. DOI 10.5589/m03-040** [CITED-IN Hopkinson 2005] Peace-Athabasca Delta: graminoid +0.07 m (sd 0.15), willow scrub +0.15 m (sd 0.26). The northern-wetland anchor Hopkinson 2005 compares itself to.

**Rosso, P.H., Ustin, S.L., Hastings, A. (2006). Use of lidar to study changes associated with Spartina invasion in San Francisco Bay marshes. RSE 100(3), 295-306.** [ABS] "Lidar did not reach the ground underneath vegetation"; errors between flight lines and between returns. No magnitude in the abstract. **Sadro, S., Gastil-Buhl, M., Melack, J. (2007). Characterizing patterns of plant distribution in a southern California salt marsh using remotely sensed topographic and hyperspectral data and local tidal fluctuations. RSE 110(2), 226-239. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2007.02.024** [ABS via mirror] "The incidence of LiDAR penetration of plant canopy cover (i.e., registration of ground elevation) was only three percent"; species-specific corrections cut overall uncertainty 58 %; dominant plant classes within a 17 cm elevation band. Relevance: the 3 % is the only published PENETRATION FRACTION for a short closed canopy before Gould 2013; Elba's 1-4 % sits on it.

**Reutebuch, S.E., McGaughey, R.J., Andersen, H.-E., Carson, W.W. (2003). Accuracy of a high-resolution lidar terrain model under a conifer forest canopy. Canadian J. Remote Sensing 29(5), 527-535.** [FULL; UW forsys PDF] 4 pulses/m2, 40 cm footprint, 347 surveyed points; overall DTM error +0.22 +/- 0.24 m; clearcut +0.16 (sd 0.23, range -0.48 to +0.61); the authors treat the clearcut 0.16 m as "the system bias." Relevance: a CLEARCUT with slash and regrowth, not bare soil, reads +16 cm, and the paper books it as instrument bias; the 2008-era habit of treating the open-ground offset as datum rather than as low vegetation.

**Alaska DGGS (2025). Jago River lidar, Raw Data File 2025-030.** [FULL; dggs.alaska.gov PDF] UAV lidar, 41.1 pts/m2, 8.2 cm pulse spacing; after a +1.5 cm constant correction the mean offset is +1.8 cm on 37 non-vegetated and +13.1 cm on 67 vegetated checkpoints (tundra); RMSE 8.7 vs 18.6 cm. The companion APEX/Bonanza Creek release (rdf2026-017, [ABS]) reports +12.5 cm over boardwalks, bogs, tussocks and tundra. Relevance: tundra and tussock at 41 pts/m2 give the SAME +13 cm as Elba at 17 pulses/m2; density alone does not buy the ground.

**MacDonell, C.J., Williams, R.D., Maniatis, G., Roberts, K., Naylor, M. (2023). Consumer-grade UAV solid-state LiDAR accurately quantifies topography in a vegetated fluvial environment. Earth Surf. Process. Landf. 48(11). DOI 10.1002/esp.5608** [FULL; Glasgow eprint] DJI Zenmuse L1 on a Matrice 300 RTK, River Feshie, Scotland; GNSS residual -0.015 m on gravel, "0.007 m" on moss, 0.290 m over heather, 0.116 m over grass ("most attributable to the lesser density of the vegetation structure"); cross-sections show "the lack of penetration of LiDAR measurements through dense vegetation (e.g. heather)"; conclusion: "unable to penetrate dense ground-hugging vegetation like heather or thick grass, resulting in elevation bias." Relevance: a 2023 UAV sensor at hundreds of points/m2 reads a grass sward 0.12 m high and heather 0.29 m high; the Elba 0.13 m is the UAV-era grass number too.

**Meng, X., Currit, N., Zhao, K. (2010). Ground filtering algorithms for airborne LiDAR data: a review of critical issues. Remote Sensing 2(3), 833-860. DOI 10.3390/rs2030833** [FULL; mdpi-res PDF downloaded] Names low vegetation as one of three domains where ground filters fail, verbatim: filters are suboptimal over "surfaces with rough terrain or discontinuous slope, dense forest areas that laser beams cannot penetrate, and regions with low vegetation that is often ignored by ground filters"; "Shrubs are common error points mis-labeled as ground surfaces. Shrub heights are usually less than one meter and so slope and elevation difference between the shrub and neighboring ground points are similar to those between ground points and neighboring ground points in variable terrain"; and the closing call for "comparative studies and uncertainty analysis ... in more specialized areas such as low vegetation and coastal areas." Relevance: the review frames low vegetation as a FILTER problem (points mislabeled), which presumes ground returns exist to be found; it has no category for the case where no pulse reaches the soil.

### B. Correction methods beyond the marsh core already in the repo

**Cooper, H.M., Zhang, C., Davis, S.E., Troxler, T.G. (2019). Object-based correction of LiDAR DEMs using RTK-GPS data and machine learning modeling in the coastal Everglades. Environmental Modelling & Software 112, 179-191. DOI 10.1016/j.envsoft.2018.11.003 [DOI UNVERIFIED]** [FULL; NSF-PAR PDF] Two Everglades sites, sawgrass (Cladium) and mangrove/buttonwood; lidar ~2 pts/m2; 483 RTK points (RMSE <= 0.03 m) collected November-May to match the lidar season; OBIA segments of 0.30 m aerial imagery with per-object lidar statistics (min, mean, max, sd, range, count) and spectral means as predictors; RF, SVM, kNN, ANN vs the common bias-correction ("BIN"). Random forest: Nine Mile MBE 0.18 -> -0.02 m, RMSE 0.22 -> 0.08 m; Flamingo MBE 0.17 -> 0.02 m, RMSE 0.24 -> 0.10 m; a Monte Carlo error model gives per-object uncertainty. Relevance: the per-object lidar MINIMUM and SD enter as predictors next to the spectral bands, i.e. a 2019 machine-learning version of texture-plus-imagery; residual 8-10 cm, again needing local RTK. <!-- spelling: ok (verbatim title) -->

**Munoz, D.F., Cissell, J.R., Moftakhari, H. (2019). Adjusting emergent herbaceous wetland elevation with object-based image analysis, random forest and the 2016 NLCD. Remote Sensing 11(20), 2346. DOI 10.3390/rs11202346** [FULL; mdpi-res PDF; the brief's "Cooper 2019 RS 11:2346" conflated this paper with Cooper 2019 EMS] Fire Island NY, Weeks Bay AL, Savannah GA. The correction is NOT RTK-trained: emergent-herbaceous-wetland cells (mapped by OBIA + RF on Landsat surface reflectance with the 2016 NLCD) are linearly rescaled into the tidal frame, z_adj = (u - l)(z_raw - z_min)/(z_max - z_min) + l, with l the upper midpoint of the tidal frame (MSL + half the upper tide range) and u = MHW + upper tide range from the nearest NOAA gauge (Table 5: Fire Island l = 0.052 m, u = 0.511 m, z_max 1.035 m). Validation against 1,530 public RTK points on Fire Island (Table 8): z_raw - RTK ME 0.250 m (max +1.319, min -0.926); z_adj - RTK ME 0.003 m; "the mean elevation of z_raw (~0.50 m) clearly overestimates the true mean RTK elevation (~0.25 m) by a factor of two"; "Fire Island showed maximum overestimation (up to 0.35 m)"; the authors note the rescaling "also modifies the natural spread of marsh elevation by narrowing its standard deviation." (The repo note's RMSE 0.30 -> 0.08 m came from a reader proxy; this PDF's Table 8 SE/RMSE columns are garbled in extraction and are not quoted here.) Relevance: a correction that needs NO ground truth, because a tidal marsh platform has an external physical elevation constraint (the tidal frame); a floodplain hay meadow has no such constraint, which is why the Elba case needs the RTK points.

**Alizad, K., Medeiros, S.C., Foster-Martinez, M.R., Hagen, S.C. (2020). Model sensitivity to topographic uncertainty in meso- and microtidal marshes. IEEE JSTARS 13, 807-814. DOI 10.1109/JSTARS.2020.2973490** [ABS; Crossref-confirmed via DOAJ] Not a correction paper: it propagates the uncorrected vs RTK-adjusted lidar DEM through a marsh model; at the microtidal sites (Apalachicola, Grand Bay) lidar-only topography gives kappa 0.1 against NWI, RTK-adjusted 0.6-0.77; mesotidal Plum Island 0.73 unadjusted. The "successor" to LEAN the brief asked about is therefore a consequence study; the LEAN method itself was extended operationally by USGS (LEAN-corrected DEMs for Suisun Marsh: initial ME 22.5 cm sd 17.5, RMSE 28.5 -> ME 0, sd 9.7, RMSE 9.7 cm; Collier County) [ABS, USGS data releases]. **Medeiros, Bobinsky & Abdelwahab 2022** (already in repo) is the Sentinel-2 RF successor; the 2021 Embry-Riddle posters on the Pascagoula (Sentinel-2A + RF) are its precursors [ABS].

**Mucke, W., Briese, C., Hollaus, M. (2010). Terrain echo probability assignment based on full-waveform airborne laser scanning observables. ISPRS XXXVIII-7B, 193-198.** [FULL; isprs.org] Leithagebirge monastery site, Riegl LMS-Q680 FWF ALS, ~20 last echoes/m2, December 2009 leaf-off, co-registered with 16 TLS stations; the echo width is modeled probabilistically against amplitude rather than thresholded (Doneus & Briese 2006 / Doneus et al. 2008 used a hard echo-width threshold to drop low-vegetation echoes before DTM filtering). Observations: ground echoes are narrow; echoes in dense low understory are broadened; in dense canopy a weak last echo "was not accepted in the echo detection step." No accuracy table in this paper. **Doneus, M., Briese, C., Fera, M., Janner, M. (2008). Archaeological prospection of forested areas using full-waveform airborne laser scanning. J. Archaeological Science 35(4), 882-893. DOI 10.1016/j.jas.2007.06.013** [ABS]. **Wagner, W., Hollaus, M., Briese, C., Ducic, V. (2008). 3D vegetation mapping using small-footprint full-waveform airborne laser scanners. ## Synthesis

**What exists.** Five families of correction, every one calibrated on survey ground truth. (1) Cover-class offsets: Hladik & Alber 2012 (RMSE 0.16 -> 0.10 m), Sadro 2007, Gopfert 2006 (+8 to +24 cm), Pfeifer 2004 (dense grass +7.3 cm). (2) Regression on an external index: LEAN (Buffington 2016, RMSE 0.072 m), Medeiros 2022, Cooper 2019 (RMSE 0.22 -> 0.08 m). (3) Regression on the laser's own height texture: Pfeifer 2004, Gopfert 2007 (R2 0.35-0.47), which FAILS on grass under 20-30 cm at 1 pt/m2 (Gorte 2005: "Vegetation causes noise rather than spatial patterns"). (4) Low order statistics: minimum-bin (Rogers 2016 0.14 -> 0.09 m; Ewald 2013 still 36-49 cm under Carex), block-minimum filters (Tinkham 2011), window tuning (Wang 2009). (5) Routes around the problem: tidal-frame rescaling with no RTK (Munoz 2019, ME 0.25 -> 0.003 m), full-waveform rejection of broadened echoes (Doneus 2008, Mucke 2010), and UAV lidar at hundreds of points/m2, which still reads grass 0.12 m high (MacDonell 2023) and tundra +13 cm (Alaska DGGS 2025). The published floor is 7-10 cm RMSE, and only where fitted (Medeiros 2022: no transfer across season or site).

**What applies at Elba.** With 1-4 % penetration, a 0.5 m footprint and a 0.5 m vendor minimum return separation (0.63 m observed), soil and sward merge into one echo (Wagner 2004, Ullrich 2011); no filter, percentile or waveform trick recovers a surface the pulse did not resolve, and the lowest return in 5 m sits 5 cm up (Gould 2013: 5 of 15,703 returns at the ground under ceanothus). The supported routes are RTK-calibrated: a per-field class offset (0.13 m, checked against the vendor's -0.09 m vegetated checkpoints) or a LEAN-type regression on Sentinel-2 NDVI or on the cloud's near-ground fraction (`lowveg`, the Pfeifer-Gorte texture idea on a 17x denser cloud). The 2008 flight is Gorte 2005's case, ~1 pt/m2 over an autumn sward under 0.4 m, whose 0-15 cm shift was real but not predictable from the cloud.

**Not done.** Nobody has (a) counted pulses reaching the soil in a grass sward against density, or derived how many pulses a cell needs for its minimum to touch ground (Roussel 2017 did the canopy top only); (b) measured a hay-meadow or reed-canary-grass offset; (c) tested whether a 13 cm sward shifts the single 1,064 nm echo in a way the TerrainMapper's waveform attributes could read; (d) used the per-cell ground-return FRACTION as the offset predictor; or (e) corrected a leaf-off 1 pt/m2 flight from a later 17 pt/m2 flight of the same sward.
