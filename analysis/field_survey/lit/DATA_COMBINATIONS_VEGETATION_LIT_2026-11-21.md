## Question

At the Elba floodplain (Whitewater River, MN) the project holds, per 1 m cell: discrete-return lidar
structure metrics from the May 2021 3DEP flight (return-height distribution shape, penetration share,
top height, intensity mean and CV; ~17 pulses/m^2, 1,064 nm, green-up); a leaf-off November 2008
flight at ~1 pulse/m^2; Sentinel-2 and NAIP phenology (green-up, summer, autumn, senescence timing);
and 147 RTK ground points. Target: vegetation presence, structure and type on floodplains, reed
canary grass (Phalaris arundinacea) the species of interest. Andy asks which COMBINATIONS of data
have been used to understand vegetation presence and structure: lidar structure + optical phenology,
lidar + radar, multi-date or multi-season lidar, lidar + field structure, spaceborne lidar (GEDI,
ICESat-2) + Landsat/Sentinel, and especially combinations aimed at SHORT vegetation (grassland, hay
meadow, marsh, crops, shrub, riparian herbs). Methods: feature-level fusion in random forests,
physically based models taking lidar and optical together, and any use of a leaf-off + leaf-on lidar
pair as a phenological measurement.

## Findings by paper

### A. Reviews and frameworks for combining lidar with other sensors

**Torabzadeh, H., Morsdorf, F., Schaepman, M.E. (2014). Fusion of imaging spectroscopy and airborne laser scanning data for characterization of forest ecosystems: a review. ISPRS J. Photogramm. Remote Sens. 97, 25-35. DOI 10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2014.08.001** [FULL via the identical chapter 2 of Torabzadeh's 2015 UZH dissertation; the ZORA copy is behind a bot wall; Crossref-verified] Forest-only by title and scope. Verbatim from the chapter abstract: "We classified the approaches by fusion level (data or product level) and by choice of methods (physical or empirical methods). Five different categories of products (landcover maps, aboveground biomass, biophysical parameters, gross/net primary productivity and biochemical parameters), have been found ... A qualitative accuracy analysis of the products exposed that currently landcover maps are profiting the most from ALS and IS data fusion, while there is room for improvements in respect to the other products, such as biophysical parameters. Only few studies using physical approaches were found, but we expect the use of such approaches will increase with the growing availability of physically based radiative transfer models that can simulate both, ALS and IS data." Their Fig. 2.4 counts: empirical ~20, physical 2, hybrid 3, other 1 studies by method; data-level 10, product-level 15, multi-level 23 by fusion level. They reject the pixel/feature/decision taxonomy for ALS ("since ALS originally delivers non-imagery datasets") and propose data level (ALS gridded to height statistics, DSM/DTM/CHM, stacked with the spectral bands), product level (each sensor converted to an ecosystem parameter first, then combined) and multi-level. On physical fusion: "physical models for LiDAR data are only a quite recent development, in particular for small footprint ALS data ... small-footprint ALS data can be used in conjunction with these models by aggregating small footprint data to larger footprint (e.g. Koetz et al., 2007b), but losing many of the inherent advantages of the small-footprint information"; "until now, the absence of proper physical models has led to physically based fusion approaches being used only at the product level." Relevance: the taxonomy to use for Elba is data-level fusion (per-cell lidar shape metrics and Sentinel-2/NAIP phenometrics in one random forest); the review says that is where land-cover products gain most, that biophysical retrievals gain least, and that no physical model fuses small-footprint discrete returns with spectra. Nothing in it concerns vegetation under 2 m.

**Lefsky, M.A., Cohen, W.B., Parker, G.G., Harding, D.J. (2002). Lidar remote sensing for ecosystem studies. BioScience 52(1), 19-30. DOI 10.1641/0006-3568(2002)052[0019:LRSFES]2.0.CO;2** [ABS; OUP page; Crossref-verified] The abstract states that lidar "directly measures the three-dimensional distribution of plant canopies" and "can accurately estimate vegetation structural attributes"; the opening says passive optical (aerial photography, Landsat TM) and radar (RADARSAT) "have proven to be satisfactory for many ecological applications" but their accuracy falls as biomass and LAI rise and they give only two-dimensional images. No fusion recipe, no short vegetation. It is the standard citation for the complementarity argument (lidar = structure, optical = composition/condition) and nothing more for this question.

**Jin, H., Mountrakis, G. (2022). Fusion of optical, radar and waveform LiDAR observations for land cover classification. ISPRS J. Photogramm. Remote Sens. 187, 171-190. DOI 10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2022.03.010** [FULL; NSF-PAR accepted manuscript] A review table of ~40 fusion studies with their reported gains (e.g. Adams et al. shrubland mapping, Landsat-8 + ALS50, RF, +9 % OA; Forzieri et al. Marecchia River, +5-18 % OA; Geerling 2007 listed at +6-27 % OA / +0.06-0.28 kappa; Fu et al. 2017 Sanjiang Plain wetland vegetation, Gaofen-1 + ALOS-1 RF, -7 to +37 % OA; Cai et al. 2020 Dongting Lake, S2 + MODIS + S1, +7 % OA), and a Central New York case study: multitemporal Landsat-5 TM (leaf-on, leaf-off, two- and four-date), multitemporal ALOS PALSAR, and LVIS waveform rh25/rh50/rh75/rh100 (20 m footprints, 1,064 nm), seven classes incl. shrubland and herbaceous/planted, random forest, 1,400 reference pixels. Verbatim: "the combined spectral, scattering and vertical structural information provided the maximum discriminative capability among different land cover types, giving rise to the highest overall accuracy of 83% (2-19% and 9-35% superior to the two-sensor and single-sensor scenarios with overall accuracies of 64-81% and 48-74%, respectively). Greater improvement was achieved when combining multitemporal Landsat images with LVIS-derived canopy height metrics as opposed to PALSAR features, suggesting that LVIS contributed more useful thematic information complementary to spectral data and beneficial to the classification task, especially for vegetation classes." Also: multitemporal Landsat alone beat single-date Landsat by 10 % OA; multitemporal PALSAR beat single-date PALSAR by 14-15 %. Relevance: the one controlled three-sensor experiment with a herbaceous class; the ranking is optical time series > lidar height > L-band radar as the second source, and the lidar term is RH height of a 20 m footprint, i.e. a tall-vegetation discriminator. Standard errors of OA ~2 %.

### B. Spaceborne lidar + optical: the canopy-height line and its floor for short vegetation

**Dubayah, R., et al. (2020). The Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation: high-resolution laser ranging of the Earth's forests and topography. Science of Remote Sensing 1, 100002. DOI 10.1016/j.srs.2020.100002** [ABS; OpenAlex; already cited in the return-shape note for RH metrics] "3 lasers that produce 8 transects ... over 10 billion waveforms at a footprint resolution of 25 m ... canopy height, canopy foliar profiles, Leaf Area Index (LAI), sub-canopy topography and biomass ... for fusion using radar and other remote sensing instruments." 51.6 degrees N limit (Elba at 44.1 N is inside). **Neuenschwander, A., Pitts, K. (2019). The ATL08 land and vegetation product for the ICESat-2 Mission. RSE 221, 247-259. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2018.11.005** [ABS; Semantic Scholar] Photon-counting ATLAS; ATL08 gives terrain and canopy heights per 100 m segment after signal/noise separation; "predicated uncertainties for terrain and canopy heights using two simulated ATLAS data sets." Neither paper gives a short-vegetation accuracy.

**Potapov, P., Li, X., Hernandez-Serna, A., Tyukavina, A., Hansen, M.C., Kommareddy, A., et al. (2021). Mapping global forest canopy height through integration of GEDI and Landsat data. RSE 253, 112165. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2020.112165** [FULL; Zenodo preprint of 2020, same numbers as the published abstract] GEDI RH95 (cross-validated metric choice) gridded to 30 m Landsat ARD metrics, per-tile bagged regression trees; forest defined as "vegetation with the height of 3 meters or above"; "The global forest height map was compared to the GEDI validation data (RMSE = 6.6m; MAE = 4.45m, R2 = 0.62) and available airborne lidar data (RMSE = 9.07m; MAE = 6.36m, R2 = 0.61)"; "the mean underestimation was greater within short (3m) and tall forests (above 21m height)"; non-forest training was oversampled 1.5x "to reduce effect of RH95 overestimation"; "GEDI data overestimate forest height on slopes within temperate and subtropical mountain grasslands, e.g. in New Zealand and Lesotho." Relevance: the product has no information below 3 m by construction, and its known failure is grassland on slopes reading as forest.

**Lang, N., Kalischek, N., Armston, J., Schindler, K., Dubayah, R., Wegner, J.D. (2022). Global canopy height regression and uncertainty estimation from GEDI LIDAR waveforms with deep ensembles. RSE 268, 112760. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2021.112760** [ABS; OpenAlex] CNN ensemble on the raw GEDI waveform regresses canopy-top height with "an expected RMSE of 2.7 m with low bias" against airborne (G-LiHT/LVIS) reference. **Lang, N., Jetz, W., Schindler, K., Wegner, J.D. (2023). A high-resolution canopy height model of the Earth. Nature Ecol. Evol. 7, 1778-1789. DOI 10.1038/s41559-023-02206-6** [FULL; arXiv 2204.08322 version] GEDI RH98 (from the 2022 model) as sparse labels, Sentinel-2 L2A 10 m bands as input, CNN ensemble; validation on held-out Sentinel-2 tiles (20 %). Verbatim: "The average root mean square error (aRMSE) of the height estimates, balanced across all 5-meter height intervals, is 7.3 m, and the average mean error (aME, i.e. bias) is -1.8 m ... The overall RMSE over all validation samples (without height balancing) is 6.0 m, with a bias of 1.3 m. The latter is due to a slight overestimation of low canopy heights, and is the price we pay for improving the performance on rare tall canopies"; "The model is able to correctly identify bare soil in deserts with zero height, with marginal error and no bias. The bias is also low in montane, temperate, and tropical grasslands ... but higher in flooded grasslands. The most severe overestimation, on average ~2.5 m is observed for mangroves, tundra and tropical coniferous forests." Against independent LVIS: RMSE 7.8 m, ME 0.6 m. ### D. Lidar + radar

Covered by Jin & Mountrakis 2022 (section A: LVIS height beat PALSAR as the complement to Landsat; three sensors 83 % vs best two 81 %), Fassnacht 2021 (section C: TanDEM-X and lidar heights selected for woody types, not grassland), Bourgeau-Chavez 2013/2015 (spectral-fusion note F09: PALSAR + Landsat Phragmites, no lidar) and Qi et al. 2019 RSE 221:621-634 (simulated GEDI + TanDEM-X InSAR for FOREST height, 10.1016/j.rse.2018.11.035, [bibliographic]). Estimates of the standard deviation and skewness of height differed more strongly, especially for Algerian oaks which experienced considerable leaf expansion." **Cushman, K.C., Armston, J., Dubayah, R., Duncanson, L., Hancock, S., Janik, D., et al. DOI 10.1088/1748-9326/acd2ec** [ABS; Crossref, verbatim] Drone lidar leaf-off and leaf-on 51 days apart, Czech mountain forest; "Mean canopy cover increased by 31% from leaf-off to leaf-on conditions, from 57% to 88%. RH metrics < RH50 were more sensitive to changes in leaf phenology than RH metrics >= RH50"; AGBD models trained leaf-on differ by 0.6-19 % when applied to leaf-off. Relevance of the pair: the SHAPE statistics of the return distribution (SD, skewness, the lower RH metrics) are the season-sensitive ones, which is the same statement as Andy's 2008-vs-2021 difference (broad 0.2-0.4 m population vs narrow 0.13 m peak) made in forests.

**Mandlburger, G., Wieser, M., Hollaus, M., Pfennigbauer, M., Riegl, U. (2016). Multi-temporal UAV-borne LiDAR point clouds for vegetation analysis: a case study. Geophysical Research Abstracts 18, EGU2016-7036-1.** [FULL; one-page abstract] Riegl VUX-1UAV over a Pielach River alluvial forest, February and 28 October 2015, same shot count: ground density 517 -> 267 points/m2, upper canopy 348 -> 757, shrub layer 0.15-3 m 178 -> 259 points/m2 (+46 %); "A sharp ground point density drop could be observed in areas covered by an invasive weed species (Fallopia japonica) which keeps its extremely dense foliage till late in the year." Relevance: the only lidar two-season measurement found in which an INVASIVE HERB's late phenology is read directly as a ground-return-density change between flights, at UAV density.

**Shi, Y., Wang, T., Kissling, W.D. (2025). Multi-temporal high-resolution data products of ecosystem structure derived from country-wide airborne laser scanning surveys of the Netherlands. ESSD 17, 3641-3677. DOI 10.5194/essd-17-3641-2025** [ABS; Crossref, verbatim] AHN1-AHN4 (1996-2022), ~70 TB, "25 lidar-derived vegetation metrics at 10 m" per epoch; the multi-epoch national product that a Dutch floodplain study could difference, but the epochs are years apart and mixed-season, as at Elba. (Wood et al. 2022 RS 14:1290 [FULL, Montana rangeland] is UAV OPTICAL phenology, +5-10 % OA from four flights over one; no lidar.)

**Verdict for E.** No crewed-airborne study differences a leaf-off and a green-up lidar flight over herbaceous ground as a phenological measurement; the published two-season designs are forest understory (Hill & Broughton, Brubaker), tree species intensity (Kim), riparian woody structure (Wasser), within-spring leaf expansion (Simonson, Cushman), one UAV alluvial-forest shrub layer (Mandlburger) and the Hungarian full-waveform grassland (Zlinszky 2014, in repo). The floodplain herbaceous two-season height profile exists only in UAV photogrammetry (van Iersel 2016/2018), where the February surface is the datum.

### F. Physically based models taking lidar and optical together

**Koetz, B., Sun, G., Morsdorf, F., Ranson, K.J., Kneubuhler, M., Itten, K., Allgower, B. (2007). Fusion of imaging spectrometer and LIDAR data over combined radiative transfer models for forest canopy characterization. RSE 106, 449-459. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2006.09.013** [FULL via the publication chapter of Koetz's 2006 UZH dissertation; ZORA blocked] Two linked RTMs, GeoSAIL (spectra) and the Sun & Ranson 3-D waveform model (large-footprint LVIS-type lidar), inverted by look-up table on a SYNTHETIC coniferous data set from the ZELIG growth model over ten soil types. Table 4 (overall): from LIDAR alone, tree height R2 0.97 / RMSE 1.46 m, crown extension 0.97 / 1.72 m, fractional cover 0.92 / 0.08; from the COMBINED inversion, LAI 0.80 / 1.15, fractional cover 0.90 / 0.07, chlorophyll 0.80 / 5.92 ug/cm2, water 0.76 / 0.0058 mg/cm2. The text: "The improvement of the combined spectral and waveform information dimensions was subsequently evaluated relative to the spectral information performance"; lidar "can improve the accuracy and robustness of canopy parameter retrieval by reducing uncertainties related to the canopy structure. On the other hand, the accurate interpretation of the LIDAR signal depends on the spectral properties of canopy elements as well as the background. The two sensors ... are thus mutually dependant." Relevance: the canonical physical fusion; forest, large footprint, synthetic validation; the structural prior from lidar is what lets the spectral inversion resolve LAI from cover. No short-canopy analog exists.

**Gastellu-Etchegorry, J.-P., Yin, T., Lauret, N., Grau, E., Rubio, J., Cook, B.D., et al. (2016). Simulation of satellite, airborne and terrestrial LiDAR with DART (I): waveform simulation with quasi-Monte Carlo ray tracing. RSE 184, 418-435. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2016.07.010; Yin, T., Lauret, N., Gastellu-Etchegorry, J.-P. (2016). (II): ALS and TLS multi-pulse acquisitions, photon counting, and solar noise. RSE 184, 454-468. DOI 10.1016/j.rse.2016.07.009** [bibliographic; Crossref-verified] DART simulates passive optical images and lidar waveforms/discrete returns from the SAME 3-D scene, which is the tool the Torabzadeh review said was missing. **Luo, Y., Xie, D., Qi, J., Zhou, K., Yan, G., Mu, X. (2023). LESS LiDAR: a full-waveform and discrete-return multispectral LiDAR simulator based on ray tracing algorithm. Remote Sensing 15, 4529. DOI 10.3390/rs15184529** [bibliographic + search snippet] the LESS counterpart (waveforms within 1 % of DART on an abstract scene; multispectral lidar + NDVI from the same scene). **Hmida, S.B., Kallel, A., Gastellu-Etchegorry, J.-P., Roujean, J.-L. (2017). Crop biophysical properties estimation based on LiDAR full-waveform inversion using the DART RTM. IEEE JSTARS 10, 4853-4868. DOI 10.1109/jstars.2017.2763242** [bibliographic; HAL blocked] DART look-up-table inversion of full waveforms over maize and wheat mock-ups, lidar only. Relevance: the forward machinery for a joint simulation of a sward (a DART scene of 0.15-0.4 m stalks over soil, run for a 1,064 nm discrete return with a 4 ns pulse AND for Sentinel-2 reflectance) exists and has been applied to crops for lidar alone; nobody has run it for a grass sward with both sensors, and North et al. 2010 (FLIGHT, in the theory note) is the GLAS-forest version.

### G. Invasive plants from fused data (beyond the grasses already in the repo)

**Singh, K.K., Davis, A.J., Meentemeyer, R.K. (2015). Detecting understory plant invasion in urban forests using LiDAR. IJAEOG 38, 267-279. DOI 10.1016/j.jag.2015.01.012** [ABS-snippet + Semantic Scholar TLDR; Crossref-verified] Chinese privet (Ligustrum sinense) under canopy, Charlotte NC; lidar metrics grouped as overstory, understory, topography, overall vegetation; RF and logistic regression; IKONOS spectral. "The results demonstrate the superiority of LiDAR-derived metrics over spectral data and fusion of LiDAR and spectral data for accurately mapping the spatial distribution of the forest understory invader"; RF on the lidar TOPOGRAPHY metrics gave the highest accuracy. The companion Singh et al. 2018 ISPRS J. 142:151-161 (10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2018.05.023) uses intra-annual (leaf-off window) phenology for the same invader. **Chance, C.M., Coops, N.C., Plowright, A.A., Tooke, T.R., Christen, A., Aven, N. DOI 10.3389/fpls.2016.01528** [ABS; OpenAlex, verbatim] Himalayan blackberry and English ivy, Surrey BC; "LiDAR-derived predictor variables included irradiance models, canopy structural characteristics, and orographic variables. RF detection accuracy ranged from 77.8% to 87.8% for Himalayan blackberry and 81.9% to 82.1% for English ivy, with open areas classified more accurately than areas under canopy cover." ## Synthesis

**Which combinations have worked for short vegetation.** Three, each with a measured gain. (1) A canopy height next to spectra in one random forest: Hall & Lara 2022 took 4-band multispectral on a tall-grass prairie from 27 to 45 % (species) and 43 to 61 % (PFT) with a UAV-lidar CHM, then to 52 % with phenometrics, while hyperspectral alone sat at 73/86 % and gained 5/3 points from the CHM; Van Iersel 2018 held 94-98 % OA on a Waal floodplain with photogrammetric height in the model and 86 % without it; Abeysinghe 2019 took Phragmites from 87.7 to 93.5 % with October NDVI plus a CHM. (2) Elevation as hydroperiod: Hladik 2013, Zhang 2014 and Van Alphen 2024 found the lidar DTM, not a vegetation metric, the most diagnostic lidar term for marsh communities. (3) Biomass: structure beats spectra at TLS density (Schulze-Bruninghoff 2021, 0.75 vs 0.60, fused 0.81) and loses at UAV-lidar density (Ma 2026, herbaceous 0.24 vs 0.87, fused 0.88; Fassnacht 2021 dropped every height metric for grassland). Complements rank optical time series, lidar height, then L-band radar (Jin & Mountrakis 2022). The spaceborne products start at 3 m with a +1.3 m low-canopy bias and flooded grasslands a problem biome (Potapov 2021; Lang 2023); ICESat-2 reaches ~1 m RMSE on 0-5 m canopies (Zhu 2023), enough to flag shrub and reed, not a sward.

**What each source contributes.** Lidar gives a height or a ground elevation; in every short-vegetation paper the lidar term that mattered was one of those two, and intensity entered once (Chen 2023). Optical bands give composition; phenometrics add 2-10 points on top of bands and were the whole signal in the Wisconsin RCG maps. Radar added least wherever lidar was present.

**A leaf-off plus green-up lidar pair as phenology.** Not for herbaceous ground from crewed aircraft. The two-season designs are forest understory and species (Hill & Broughton 2009; Kim 2009; Brubaker 2018; Wasser 2013), within-spring leaf expansion (Simonson 2018; Cushman 2023: spread, skewness and the lower RH metrics are the season-sensitive statistics), one UAV shrub layer (Mandlburger 2016), and Zlinszky 2014 (in repo). The floodplain herbaceous two-season height profile exists only in UAV photogrammetry (van Iersel 2016/2018), with the February surface taken as ground and the authors' warning that senescent vegetation still stands on it.

**Not in the literature relative to Elba.** No paper fuses per-cell discrete-return SHAPE metrics (mode offset, spread, ground share) with satellite phenology for any herbaceous type; the lidar term is always a CHM or DTM. No crewed two-epoch lidar pair over a hay meadow or marsh is used as a phenological measurement, and none has RTK soil under both epochs. No physical model has been run jointly for a 1,064 nm discrete return and Sentinel-2 reflectance over a sward, though DART and LESS simulate both from one scene and Hmida 2017 did the lidar half for crops. No lidar-fusion detection of reed canary grass, cheatgrass, cogongrass or Spartina exists; the invasive-grass precedents (Levick 2015; Marcinkowska-Ochtyra 2018) rest on a height gap or on hyperspectral bands, neither of which RCG against brome offers.
